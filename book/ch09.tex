\Cref{ch:ch08} built networks and ran them forward with weights chosen by hand.
That is enough to understand what a network computes, but no one chooses a
million weights by hand. This chapter supplies the learning algorithm --- which,
at the level of ideas, is the gradient descent of \cref{ch:ch03} applied to the
cost of \cref{ch:ch06} --- and the three extensions that make it work in practice:
better activation functions, an output layer for many classes, and an efficient
way to compute the gradient with respect to every weight at once.

\section{Training in Three Steps}
\label{sec:train-steps}

Training any model in this book has had the same three steps, and a network is
no exception.

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{2.6cm}L{4.3cm}L{4.3cm}@{}}
\toprule
step & logistic regression & neural network\\
\midrule
1.\ specify $f$ & $\fvwb(\vx) = \gsig(\vw\cdot\vx + b)$
   & \code{Sequential([Dense(...), ...])}\\
2.\ specify the loss and cost & logistic loss, $J = \frac1m\sum_i\loss$
   & \code{compile(loss=BinaryCrossentropy())}\\
3.\ minimise $J$ & gradient descent on $\vw$, $b$ & \code{fit(X, y, epochs=100)}\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=tfkeras, caption={Training a network: one statement per step.}, label={lst:three-steps}]
from tensorflow.keras import Sequential, Input
from tensorflow.keras.layers import Dense
from tensorflow.keras.losses import BinaryCrossentropy

# step 1: the model
model = Sequential([Input(shape=(64,)),
                    Dense(25, activation='relu'),
                    Dense(15, activation='relu'),
                    Dense(1,  activation='sigmoid')])
# step 2: the loss
model.compile(loss=BinaryCrossentropy())
# step 3: minimise the cost
history = model.fit(X, y, epochs=100)
\end{lstlisting}

The loss named in step 2 is a formula, and for a binary target it is the
logistic loss of \cref{sec:logistic-loss}, under the name used in information
theory.

\begin{defbox}{Binary cross-entropy, and epochs}
For $y \in \{0,1\}$ and output $f \in (0,1)$, the \term{binary cross-entropy} is
\[
  \loss(f, y) = -y\ln f - (1-y)\ln(1-f),
\]
and the cost $J$ averages it over the training set, now a function of every
weight and bias in every layer. For a numeric target, the squared error
$(f - y)^2$ is used instead; note that Keras's \code{MeanSquaredError} omits the
$\frac12$ of earlier chapters, which only rescales the learning rate.

An \term{epoch} is one complete pass through the training set. \code{epochs=100}
means the optimizer sees every example one hundred times.
\end{defbox}

\begin{workedexample}{The loss of N231's predictions}
N231 of \cref{sec:forward-prop} outputs $0.8094$ for $(1,2)$ and $0.0562$ for
$(-1,-1)$. Their losses under each possible label are

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}cccc@{}}
\toprule
$\vx$ & $f(\vx)$ & $\loss$ if $y=1$ & $\loss$ if $y=0$\\
\midrule
$(1,2)$   & $0.8094$ & $0.2115$ & $1.6576$\\
$(-1,-1)$ & $0.0562$ & $2.8788$ & $0.0578$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Confident and right costs little; confident and wrong costs a great deal --- the
unbounded penalty that \cref{sec:logistic-loss} showed keeps the gradient alive.
\end{workedexample}

Step 3 is gradient descent: repeat
$\Wl{l} \leftarrow \Wl{l} - \alpha\,\partial J/\partial\Wl{l}$ and
$\bl{l} \leftarrow \bl{l} - \alpha\,\partial J/\partial\bl{l}$ for every layer.
The cost now depends on thousands or millions of parameters, and the two things
this chapter must supply are an efficient way to compute all those partial
derivatives (\cref{sec:backprop}) and a better update rule than plain gradient
descent (\cref{sec:adam}). First, the activation functions, which turn out to
matter a great deal for both.

\section{Activation Functions}
\label{sec:activations}

The sigmoid has been the only activation so far. Two others do most of the work
in practice.

\begin{defbox}{ReLU and linear activations}
The \term{rectified linear unit} is $\gsig(z) = \max(0, z)$: zero for negative
inputs, the identity for positive ones. The \term{linear activation} is
$\gsig(z) = z$, often described as ``no activation''.
\end{defbox}

\subsection{Choosing the output activation}

The output activation should have the same range as the target, and the loss
comes paired with it.

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{3.0cm}L{2.0cm}L{3.2cm}L{3.0cm}@{}}
\toprule
target $y$ & output $\gsig$ & loss & example\\
\midrule
$0$ or $1$ & sigmoid & binary cross-entropy & a transaction is fraudulent\\
any real number & linear & squared error & tomorrow's temperature change\\
non-negative & ReLU & squared error & rainfall, house price\\
one of $N$ classes & softmax & cross-entropy & which digit (\cref{sec:softmax})\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{pitfall}{A mismatched output layer}
A sigmoid output can never exceed $1$, so a model predicting house prices with
one cannot be trained into usefulness however long it runs. A linear output
under binary cross-entropy can produce $f \le 0$, where $\ln f$ is undefined.
Check the output layer against the target before debugging anything else.
\end{pitfall}

\subsection{Hidden layers: ReLU by default}

For hidden layers, ReLU has displaced the sigmoid almost entirely, for two
reasons visible in \cref{fig:activations}. It is cheaper --- a comparison, not
an exponential. And it is flat on only one side: the sigmoid is flat for large
$|z|$ in both directions, so a saturated sigmoid unit passes back almost no
gradient, while a ReLU unit passes back the full gradient whenever it is active.
Networks with ReLU hidden layers train markedly faster.

\begin{workedexample}{N231 with a ReLU hidden layer}
Keep N231's weights and change only the hidden activation. The pre-activations
$\mathbf{z}^{[1]}$ are unchanged; ReLU zeros their negative entries and passes
the rest through unsquashed:

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
 & $\vx = (1,2)$ & $\vx = (-1,-1)$\\
\midrule
$\mathbf{z}^{[1]}$             & $(4,\ 6,\ -5)$ & $(-4,\ 0,\ 3)$\\
$\va^{[1]}$, sigmoid           & $(0.982,\ 0.998,\ 0.007)$ & $(0.018,\ 0.500,\ 0.953)$\\
$\va^{[1]}$, ReLU              & $(4,\ 6,\ 0)$ & $(0,\ 0,\ 3)$\\
$z^{[2]}$, ReLU                & $8 - 6 - 0 + 0.5 = 2.5$ & $0 - 0 - 9 + 0.5 = -8.5$\\
$a^{[2]}$, ReLU                & $\mathbf{0.9241}$ & $\mathbf{0.0002}$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The predictions are the same as before but more confident, because the hidden
activations are no longer capped at $1$. With labels $y = (1, 0)$ the
binary-cross-entropy cost of the batch is $(0.0789 + 0.0002)/2 = 0.039547$.
\end{workedexample}

\begin{pitfall}{Dead ReLU units}
A ReLU unit whose $z$ is negative for every training example outputs $0$ and
has gradient $0$, so gradient descent never changes its weights: it is
\emph{dead}. A large learning rate or a bad initialisation can kill many units at
once. Variants such as the \emph{leaky ReLU}, $\max(0.01z, z)$, keep a small
slope for $z < 0$ and cannot die.
\end{pitfall}

\subsection{What the non-linearity buys}

\Cref{sec:collapse} proved that without a non-linear activation any depth
collapses to one linear layer. ReLU makes the positive side of that statement
concrete. Each ReLU unit contributes one \emph{hinge}, a point at which the
slope of the network's output changes. The linear output layer adds hinges with
weights: the network with three hidden units $r(x)$, $r(x-1)$ and $r(x-2.5)$
and output weights $(1, -2, 2)$ computes
\[
  f(x) = r(x) - 2\,r(x-1) + 2\,r(x-2.5),
\]
whose slope is $1$ on $(0,1)$, $1 - 2 = -1$ on $(1, 2.5)$ and $-1 + 2 = 1$ beyond
--- a zig-zag through $f(0) = 0$, $f(1) = 1$, $f(2.5) = -0.5$, $f(4) = 1$. With
enough hidden units, sums of hinges follow any continuous curve on a bounded
interval: the universal approximation theorem of \cref{ch:ch08}, made visible.

\begin{researchspotlight}{Nair \& Hinton (2010) --- Rectified linear units}
\textbf{The problem.} Deep networks of sigmoid or tanh units were slow and
difficult to train, and their gradients shrank as they passed backwards through
saturated units.

\textbf{The idea.} Use units whose activation is $\max(0, z)$. The paper derived
them as an approximation to a sum of many sigmoid units with shifted biases,
and showed they could be used inside the restricted Boltzmann machines then
popular for pre-training.

\textbf{The result.} Rectified units gave better results on object recognition
and face verification than binary or sigmoid units, and training was faster.
Within two years, deep ReLU networks trained by plain back-propagation were
winning image-recognition competitions.

\textbf{Why it is here.} It is the default hidden activation of
\cref{sec:activations}. The two properties argued for there --- no saturation for
$z > 0$, and an exact zero for $z < 0$ that makes activations sparse --- are the
reasons it displaced the sigmoid in hidden layers.
\parencite{nair2010}
\end{researchspotlight}

\section{Multiclass Classification and Softmax}
\label{sec:softmax}

Many problems have more than two answers: which of ten digits, which of four
leaf diseases, which of several kinds of fabric defect.

\begin{defbox}{Multiclass classification, and softmax}
In \term{multiclass classification} the target takes one of $N > 2$ values, and
exactly one is correct for each example. A \term{softmax} output layer has one
unit per class. Each computes a score $z_j = \vw_j\cdot\va + b_j$, and the scores
are turned into probabilities by
\[
  a_j = \frac{e^{z_j}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{z_k}} = \Prob(y = j \mid \vx),
  \qquad j = 1,\dots,N .
\]
The outputs are positive and sum to $1$.
\end{defbox}

Softmax is unlike every activation so far: each output depends on \emph{all}
the scores, through the denominator. Raise one score and every other probability
falls. The name says what it does --- a smooth version of picking the maximum.

\begin{workedexample}{Softmax of three scores}
Take $\mathbf{z} = (2,\ 1,\ 0.1)$. Then $e^{\mathbf{z}} = (7.3891,\ 2.7183,\
1.1052)$, which sum to $11.2125$, so
\[
  \va = (0.6590,\ 0.2424,\ 0.0986).
\]
Raising $z_1$ to $3$ gives $(0.8401,\ 0.1137,\ 0.0462)$, and to $4$ gives
$(0.9346,\ 0.0465,\ 0.0189)$: both other probabilities fall although their
scores did not change.
\end{workedexample}

\subsection{Two classes give back the sigmoid}

With $N = 2$, divide numerator and denominator by $e^{z_1}$:
\[
  a_1 = \frac{e^{z_1}}{e^{z_1} + e^{z_2}} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(z_1 - z_2)}}
      = \gsig(z_1 - z_2).
\]
Two-class softmax is logistic regression on the difference of the scores; for
$\mathbf{z} = (2,\ 0.5)$ both give $0.8176$. Softmax is the natural extension of
the sigmoid, not a replacement for it.

\subsection{The loss}

\begin{defbox}{Cross-entropy loss}
For softmax outputs $\va$ and true class $y$,
\[
  \loss(\va, y) = -\ln a_y .
\]
Only the probability assigned to the correct class is penalised. With $N = 2$ it
is exactly binary cross-entropy. Keras calls it
\code{SparseCategoricalCrossentropy}: \emph{sparse} because the label is stored as
one integer rather than as a one-hot vector.
\end{defbox}

For the scores $(2, 1, 0.1)$ the loss is $0.4170$, $1.4170$ or $2.3170$
according as the true class is $1$, $2$ or $3$. The losses differ by exactly the
gaps between the scores, because
$-\ln a_y = \ln\sum_k e^{z_k} - z_y$ --- an identity that \cref{sec:stable-softmax}
puts to work.

\begin{derivation}{The gradient of softmax with cross-entropy}
Differentiate $\loss = \ln\sum_k e^{z_k} - z_y$ with respect to $z_j$. The first
term gives
\[
  \frac{\partial}{\partial z_j}\ln\sum_k e^{z_k}
  = \frac{e^{z_j}}{\sum_k e^{z_k}} = a_j ,
\]
and the second gives $-1$ if $j = y$ and $0$ otherwise. So
\[
  \boxed{\;\frac{\partial\loss}{\partial z_j} = a_j - [\,j = y\,]\;}
  \qquad\text{or, as a vector,}\qquad
  \frac{\partial\loss}{\partial\mathbf{z}} = \va - \mathbf{e}_y ,
\]
where $\mathbf{e}_y$ is the one-hot vector of the true class. This is the
multiclass version of $\partial\loss/\partial z = f - y$ from
\cref{sec:gd-logistic}: prediction minus target, again. For $\mathbf{z} = (2,
1, 0.1)$ and true class $1$ it is $(-0.3410,\ 0.2424,\ 0.0986)$, which a central
finite difference reproduces to every digit shown.

Note how much the combination simplifies. The softmax Jacobian
$\partial a_j/\partial z_k$ is a full matrix, $a_j([j=k] - a_k)$, and the
derivative of $-\ln a_y$ is $-1/a_y$; their product collapses to
$\va - \mathbf{e}_y$. Libraries exploit this by computing the softmax and the loss
together, which is also what makes the stable implementation possible.
\end{derivation}

\section{Numerically Stable Softmax}
\label{sec:stable-softmax}

The formula for softmax is two lines of code, and those two lines fail in
practice.

\subsection{Round-off}

Floating-point numbers carry a fixed number of significant digits. A small
number computed as the difference of two numbers near $1$ loses most of them.
Mathematically $2/10{,}000 = (1 + 1/10{,}000) - (1 - 1/10{,}000)$, but in double
precision the right-hand side is $0.00019999999999997797$, wrong in the
fourteenth digit; in single precision, which TensorFlow uses by default, it is
$0.00020003318786621094$, wrong in the fourth. Computing a probability and then
its logarithm invites exactly this kind of loss.

\subsection{Overflow}

Scores of several hundred are routine in an untrained or badly scaled network,
and $e^{1000}$ exceeds the largest double, about $1.8\times10^{308}$. The naive
softmax of $(1000,\ 999,\ 998)$ is $\infty/\infty$, which is \code{nan}, and one
\code{nan} in a cost corrupts every gradient after it.

The fix uses a property of softmax: adding the same constant $c$ to every score
changes nothing, because the factor $e^c$ cancels. Choose $c = \max_k z_k$. Then
every exponent is at most $0$, so nothing overflows, and the largest term is
exactly $e^0 = 1$, so the sum cannot underflow to zero. For the loss, never form
$a_y$ and then take its logarithm; use the identity
\[
  -\ln a_y = \Bigl(c + \ln\sum_k e^{z_k - c}\Bigr) - z_y .
\]
For $(1000,\ 999,\ 998)$ and true class $1$ this gives
$\ln(1 + e^{-1} + e^{-2}) = 0.4076$, computed without ever forming $e^{1000}$.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=pythonml, caption={Stable softmax and log-softmax.}, label={lst:stable-softmax}]
def softmax(Z):        # Z: (m, N), one row per example
    Zs = Z - Z.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    E = np.exp(Zs)
    return E / E.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

def log_softmax(Z):
    c = Z.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    s = np.exp(Z - c).sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    lse = c + np.log(s)
    return Z - lse

def cross_entropy(Z, y):           # y: (m,) integer labels
    return -log_softmax(Z)[np.arange(len(y)), y].mean()
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{What this means in TensorFlow}

\begin{pitfall}{The softmax output layer and the clipped loss}
There are two ways to build a multiclass output layer in Keras.
\begin{enumerate}\raggedright
  \item A softmax output layer, \code{Dense(N, activation='softmax')}, with
        the default loss: the layer outputs probabilities and the loss takes
        their logarithm. Because a probability can round to $0$, Keras clips it
        to at least $10^{-7}$ first.
  \item A linear output layer, \code{Dense(N, activation='linear')}, with
        \code{from\_logits=True} in the loss: the layer outputs the scores ---
        the \term{logits} --- and the loss applies the stable log-softmax
        itself.
\end{enumerate}
They differ on examples that are confidently wrong. For logits $(0, 50)$ and
true class $1$, the exact loss is $50$. Version 1 computes $a_1 = 1.9\times10^{-22}$,
clips it to $10^{-7}$, and reports $-\ln 10^{-7} = 16.12$ however wrong the
model is; beyond the clip the gradient is zero, so a badly wrong example stops
pulling on the weights. Version 2 reports $50$. Use version 2.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=tfkeras, caption={The recommended multiclass set-up.}, label={lst:from-logits}]
model = Sequential([Input(shape=(64,)),
                    Dense(25, activation='relu'),
                    Dense(15, activation='relu'),
                    Dense(10, activation='linear')])
loss = SparseCategoricalCrossentropy(from_logits=True)
model.compile(loss=loss)
model.fit(X, y, epochs=100)

logits = model(X_new)          # logits, not probabilities
probs = tf.nn.softmax(logits)  # if probabilities needed
yhat = np.argmax(logits, axis=1)    # argmax is unchanged
\end{lstlisting}

The same applies to binary classification: a linear output unit with
\code{BinaryCrossentropy(from\_logits=True)}, and \code{tf.nn.sigmoid} on the
logit when a probability is wanted.

\section{Multi-label Classification}
\label{sec:multilabel}

A different question has a different output layer. Given a photograph of a
street, is there a car in it? A bus? A pedestrian? Any combination may be
present, so the label is a vector such as $(1, 0, 1)$.

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{3.2cm}L{3.8cm}L{3.8cm}@{}}
\toprule
 & multiclass & multi-label\\
\midrule
labels per example & exactly one & any number\\
output layer & $N$ softmax units & $N$ sigmoid units\\
outputs sum to & $1$ & anything in $[0, N]$\\
loss & cross-entropy & binary cross-entropy per output, summed\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A multi-label network is equivalent to $N$ binary classifiers sharing their
hidden layers. Using softmax for a multi-label problem forces the classes to
compete: a confident ``car'' pushes ``pedestrian'' down even when both are
present.

\section{Back-propagation}
\label{sec:backprop}

Training needs $\partial J/\partial\theta$ for every parameter $\theta$. For a
network of a million weights, estimating each by nudging it and re-running the
network would take a million forward passes per gradient step. Back-propagation
computes all of them in one forward pass and one backward pass.

\subsection{Derivatives as sensitivities}

If increasing $w$ by a small $\varepsilon$ changes $J$ by about $k\varepsilon$,
then $\dd J/\dd w = k$. For $J = w^2$ at $w = 3$, $J(3.001) = 9.006001$, a change of
$0.006001 \approx 6\varepsilon$, matching $\dd J/\dd w = 2w = 6$. The estimate
$(J(w+\varepsilon) - J(w))/\varepsilon$ is off by a term proportional to
$\varepsilon$; the \emph{central} difference
$(J(w+\varepsilon) - J(w-\varepsilon))/2\varepsilon$ cancels it, leaving an
error proportional to $\varepsilon^2$. That estimate is too slow to train with,
but it is the standard way to test a gradient implementation.

\subsection{The computation graph}

\begin{defbox}{Computation graph}
A \term{computation graph} breaks a calculation into elementary operations, one
per node, with edges carrying intermediate values from each operation to the
next. Evaluating it left to right is the \emph{forward pass}; propagating
derivatives right to left, multiplying by each node's local derivative, is the
\emph{backward pass}. The rule that justifies the multiplication is the chain
rule: if $J$ depends on $u$ only through $v$, then
$\partial J/\partial u = (\partial J/\partial v)(\partial v/\partial u)$.
\end{defbox}

      node[above, font=\scriptsize, MLRose] {$\frac{\partial J}{\partial a} = 2$} ([yshift=5mm]a.north);
    \draw[bwd] ([yshift=5mm]a.north) to[bend right=30]
      node[above, font=\scriptsize, MLRose] {$\frac{\partial J}{\partial c} = 2$} ([yshift=5mm]c.north);
    \node[font=\scriptsize, MLRose] at (0.4, 1.5)
      {$\frac{\partial J}{\partial w} = \frac{\partial J}{\partial c}\,x = -4$};
    \node[font=\scriptsize, MLRose] at (3.75,-1.5)
      {$\frac{\partial J}{\partial b} = \frac{\partial J}{\partial a} = 2$};
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{The computation graph of $J = \tfrac12(wx + b - y)^2$ at $w = 2$,
  $b = 8$, $x = -2$, $y = 2$. Grey arrows carry values forward; red arrows carry
  derivatives backward, each node multiplying the incoming derivative by its own
  local derivative. Nudging $w$ to $2.001$ changes $J$ by $-0.003998$, matching
  $\partial J/\partial w = -4$.}
  \label{fig:comp-graph}
\end{figure}

The decisive property is reuse. $\partial J/\partial a$ is computed once and
then used for both $\partial J/\partial b$ and $\partial J/\partial c$, and through
$c$ for $\partial J/\partial w$. Every intermediate derivative is shared by
everything to its left. A graph with $N$ nodes and $P$ parameters needs about
$N + P$ steps for the backward pass; nudging each parameter would need about
$N \times P$. With $N = 10^4$ and $P = 10^5$ that is $1.1\times10^5$ against
$10^9$.

\begin{workedexample}{Two ReLU layers}
One input, one unit per layer, ReLU in both, squared-error loss:
$a^{[1]} = r(w^{[1]}x + b^{[1]})$, $a^{[2]} = r(w^{[2]}a^{[1]} + b^{[2]})$,
$J = \frac12(a^{[2]} - y)^2$, with $x = 1$, $y = 5$, $w^{[1]} = 2$, $b^{[1]} = 0$,
$w^{[2]} = 3$, $b^{[2]} = 1$.

\textbf{Forward:} $z^{[1]} = 2$, $a^{[1]} = 2$; $z^{[2]} = 7$, $a^{[2]} = 7$;
$J = \frac12(7-5)^2 = 2$.

\textbf{Backward}, with $r'(z) = 1$ because both $z > 0$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \frac{\partial J}{\partial z^{[2]}} &= a^{[2]} - y = 2, &
  \frac{\partial J}{\partial w^{[2]}} &= 2\cdot a^{[1]} = 4, &
  \frac{\partial J}{\partial b^{[2]}} &= 2,\\
  \frac{\partial J}{\partial z^{[1]}} &= 2\cdot w^{[2]} = 6, &
  \frac{\partial J}{\partial w^{[1]}} &= 6\cdot x = 6, &
  \frac{\partial J}{\partial b^{[1]}} &= 6 .
\end{aligned}
\]
Nudging each parameter by $0.001$ changes $J$ by $0.0060045$, $0.0060045$,
$0.0040020$ and $0.0020005$ --- agreeing with $6, 6, 4, 2$ to the size of the
nudge.
\end{workedexample}

\subsection{Back-propagation for a dense network}

A dense network is a computation graph whose nodes are whole layers. The same
two rules --- store every value on the way forward, multiply local derivatives
on the way back --- give matrix formulas.

\begin{derivation}{Back-propagation for one hidden layer}
Take a network with a ReLU hidden layer, a sigmoid output and binary
cross-entropy, on a batch $\Xmat$ of $m$ examples:
\[
  \mathbf{Z}^{[1]} = \Xmat\Wl{1} + \bl{1},\quad
  \mathbf{A}^{[1]} = r\bigl(\mathbf{Z}^{[1]}\bigr),\quad
  \mathbf{Z}^{[2]} = \mathbf{A}^{[1]}\Wl{2} + \bl{2},\quad
  \mathbf{A}^{[2]} = \gsig\bigl(\mathbf{Z}^{[2]}\bigr),
\]
and $J = \frac1m\sum_i\loss^{(i)}$. Write $d\mathbf{M}$ for $\partial J/\partial
\mathbf{M}$, a matrix of the same shape as $\mathbf{M}$.

\textbf{Output pre-activation.} \Cref{sec:gd-logistic} showed
$\partial\loss/\partial z = a - y$ for a sigmoid with cross-entropy; the
$\frac1m$ comes from the average:
\[
  d\mathbf{Z}^{[2]} = \tfrac1m\bigl(\mathbf{A}^{[2]} - \vy\bigr) .
\]
\textbf{Output weights.} $Z^{[2]}_{i} = \sum_k A^{[1]}_{ik}W^{[2]}_{k} + b^{[2]}$,
so $\partial Z^{[2]}_i/\partial W^{[2]}_k = A^{[1]}_{ik}$ and, summing over
examples by the chain rule,
\[
  d\Wl{2} = \mathbf{A}^{[1]\mathsf{T}}d\mathbf{Z}^{[2]},\qquad
  d\bl{2} = \textstyle\sum_i d\mathbf{Z}^{[2]}_{i} .
\]
\textbf{Back into the hidden layer.} $\partial Z^{[2]}_i/\partial A^{[1]}_{ik} =
W^{[2]}_k$, so $d\mathbf{A}^{[1]} = d\mathbf{Z}^{[2]}\mathbf{W}^{[2]\mathsf{T}}$, and the
ReLU multiplies by its local derivative, $1$ where $z > 0$ and $0$ elsewhere:
\[
  d\mathbf{Z}^{[1]} = d\mathbf{A}^{[1]} \odot \bigl[\mathbf{Z}^{[1]} > 0\bigr] .
\]
\textbf{Hidden weights}, by the same argument as the output weights:
\[
  d\Wl{1} = \Xmat^{\mathsf{T}}d\mathbf{Z}^{[1]},\qquad
  d\bl{1} = \textstyle\sum_i d\mathbf{Z}^{[1]}_{i,:} .
\]
Every gradient has the shape of its parameter --- a check worth asserting in
code. With a sigmoid hidden layer the only change is the local derivative:
$\odot\,\mathbf{A}^{[1]}\odot(1 - \mathbf{A}^{[1]})$, by \cref{sec:sigmoid}.
\end{derivation}

\begin{algorithmbox}{Back-propagation (one gradient step)}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=pseudocode]
# forward: store every Z and A
A[0] <- X
for l = 1..L:
    Z[l] <- A[l-1] @ W[l] + b[l];   A[l] <- g_l(Z[l])
# backward: output layer first
# (sigmoid or softmax output with cross-entropy)
dZ[L] <- (A[L] - Y) / m
for l = L..1:
    dW[l] <- A[l-1].T @ dZ[l]
    db[l] <- column sums of dZ[l]
    if l > 1:
        dZ[l-1] <- (dZ[l] @ W[l].T) * g'_{l-1}(Z[l-1])
# update
for l = 1..L:
    W[l] <- W[l] - alpha * dW[l]
    b[l] <- b[l] - alpha * db[l]
\end{lstlisting}
The backward loop costs about as much as the forward loop: one matrix product per
layer for $d\mathbf{W}$, and one for $d\mathbf{Z}$.
\end{algorithmbox}

\begin{workedexample}{A complete forward and backward pass}
Train N231-ReLU on the single example $\vx = (1,2)$ with $y = 1$.

\textbf{Forward} (from \cref{sec:activations}): $\mathbf{z}^{[1]} = (4, 6, -5)$,
$\va^{[1]} = (4, 6, 0)$, $z^{[2]} = 2.5$, $a^{[2]} = 0.9241$,
$\loss = -\ln 0.9241 = 0.0789$.

\textbf{Backward.}
\begin{align*}
  dz^{[2]} &= a^{[2]} - y = -0.0759\\
  d\Wl{2} &= \va^{[1]\mathsf{T}}dz^{[2]} = (-0.3034,\ -0.4551,\ 0)^{\mathsf{T}},
  \qquad db^{[2]} = -0.0759\\
  d\va^{[1]} &= dz^{[2]}\,\mathbf{W}^{[2]\mathsf{T}} = (-0.1517,\ 0.0759,\ 0.2276)\\
  d\mathbf{z}^{[1]} &= d\va^{[1]}\odot[\mathbf{z}^{[1]} > 0] = (-0.1517,\ 0.0759,\ 0)\\
  d\Wl{1} &= \vx^{\mathsf{T}}d\mathbf{z}^{[1]}
  = \begin{bmatrix}-0.1517 & 0.0759 & 0\\ -0.3034 & 0.1517 & 0\end{bmatrix},
  \qquad d\bl{1} = (-0.1517,\ 0.0759,\ 0)
\end{align*}
Read the result. The third hidden unit is off ($z = -5$), so all its incoming
gradients are exactly zero: nothing it does affects the loss. The output weight
from unit $1$ has a negative gradient, so gradient descent increases it, which
raises $z^{[2]}$ and the prediction towards $y = 1$. Every one of the thirteen
gradients agrees with a central finite difference to within $10^{-10}$. One
update with $\alpha = 1$ raises the prediction to $0.9999$ and lowers the loss to
$0.0001$.
\end{workedexample}

\begin{pitfall}{A gradient check that fails on correct code}
Run the same check on the two-example batch $\{(1,2), (-1,-1)\}$ and the largest
relative discrepancy is $6.7\times10^{-4}$ --- far too large for a correct
implementation. The implementation is correct. The second example gives the
middle hidden unit $z = 0$ exactly, at ReLU's kink. There the slope is $0$ on the
left and $1$ on the right; the central difference averages them to
$\frac12$, while back-propagation (and TensorFlow) use $0$. ReLU has no
derivative at $0$, and libraries simply pick a side. Hand-chosen integer inputs
hit the kink; real data almost never does. On twenty random inputs the same
check gives $4.1\times10^{-8}$.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{keyinsight}{You write the forward pass; the library writes the backward one}
TensorFlow and PyTorch record the operations of the forward pass as a
computation graph while it runs, and apply the backward rules automatically ---
\term{automatic differentiation}. This is why \code{model.fit} needs no
derivative from you. It does not remove the need to understand
back-propagation: vanishing gradients, dead units and exploding losses are all
statements about the backward pass, and diagnosing them requires knowing what it
computes.
\end{keyinsight}

\begin{researchspotlight}{Rumelhart, Hinton \& Williams (1986) --- Back-propagation}
\textbf{The problem.} Networks with hidden layers could represent functions that
single-layer perceptrons could not, but there was no practical way to train the
hidden units: no one knew how to assign credit or blame to a unit whose
correct output is never given.

\textbf{The idea.} Use differentiable units, and compute the derivative of the
error with respect to every weight by propagating error signals backwards
through the network with the chain rule --- the procedure of \cref{sec:backprop}.

\textbf{The result.} Networks trained this way learned useful internal
representations on their own; the paper's examples include a network that
discovered the structure of two family trees in its hidden units. The method had
precursors, including in control theory, but this paper made it the standard way
to train networks.

\textbf{Why it is here.} It is the algorithm of this section. Its central claim
--- that hidden units trained by gradient descent invent features the designer
never specified --- is the claim of \cref{sec:learned-features}, demonstrated.
\parencite{rumelhart1986}
\end{researchspotlight}

\section{Adam}
\label{sec:adam}

Plain gradient descent uses one learning rate for every parameter, and one rate
rarely suits them all. The cost $J = w_1^2 + 10w_2^2$ is steep in $w_2$ and
shallow in $w_1$. Gradient descent multiplies $w_2$ by $1 - 20\alpha$ at each
step, so it diverges for $\alpha > 0.1$, oscillates forever at $\alpha = 0.1$,
and must keep $\alpha$ below that threshold --- at which rate $w_1$, multiplied by
$1 - 2\alpha$, crawls.

\begin{defbox}{Adam}
For each parameter $w$ with gradient $d$ at step $t = 1, 2, \dots$, \term{Adam}
keeps running averages of the gradient and its square,
\[
  m \leftarrow \beta_1 m + (1-\beta_1)\,d, \qquad
  v \leftarrow \beta_2 v + (1-\beta_2)\,d^2,
\]
corrects them for having started at zero,
\[
  \hat m = \frac{m}{1-\beta_1^t}, \qquad \hat v = \frac{v}{1-\beta_2^t},
\]
and updates
\[
  w \leftarrow w - \alpha\,\frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat v} + \epsilon} .
\]
Defaults: $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$, $\epsilon = 10^{-7}$, and
$\alpha = 10^{-3}$.
\end{defbox}

When a parameter's gradient keeps its sign, $\hat m/\sqrt{\hat v} \approx \pm1$
and each step has length about $\alpha$ \emph{whatever the gradient's
magnitude}. When the gradient oscillates, $\hat m$ averages towards zero and the
steps shrink. At $t = 1$ the corrected averages are exactly $d$ and $d^2$, so the
first step is $\alpha\,\mathrm{sign}(d)$ in every coordinate: from $(-4, 1)$ with
$\alpha = 0.3$, Adam moves to $(-3.7, 0.7)$, although the gradient in $w_2$ is
$2.5$ times larger than in $w_1$.

\begin{workedexample}{Robustness to the learning rate}
Run both optimizers on $J = w_1^2 + 10w_2^2$ from $(-4, 1)$ and count the steps
until $J < 0.01$:

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrr@{}}
\toprule
$\alpha$ & gradient descent & Adam\\
\midrule
$0.003$ & $> 300$  & $> 300$\\
$0.01$  & $183$    & $> 300$\\
$0.03$  & $60$     & $259$\\
$0.09$  & $\mathbf{20}$ & $66$\\
$0.1$   & $> 300$ (oscillates) & $61$\\
$0.11$  & diverges & $78$\\
$0.3$   & diverges & $59$\\
$1$     & diverges & $\mathbf{49}$\\
$3$     & diverges & $51$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The fastest run in the table is gradient descent at $\alpha = 0.09$ --- but only
because $0.09$ sits just below the divergence threshold of $0.1$, which we know
only because we can differentiate this $J$ by hand. A little above it, gradient
descent diverges. Adam reaches the minimum for every $\alpha$ from $0.03$ to $3$,
a hundredfold range, in $49$ to $259$ steps. Its advantage is not speed on a
problem tuned by hand; it is that you do not have to tune it by hand.
\end{workedexample}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=tfkeras, caption={Adam in Keras.}, label={lst:adam}]
opt = tf.keras.optimizers.Adam(learning_rate=1e-3)
loss = SparseCategoricalCrossentropy(from_logits=True)
model.compile(optimizer=opt, loss=loss)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{researchspotlight}{Kingma \& Ba (2015) --- Adam}
\textbf{The problem.} Stochastic gradient descent on large, noisy, badly scaled
objectives needs a learning rate tuned per problem, and often per parameter.
Earlier adaptive methods each fixed part of this: momentum smoothed noisy
gradients, and AdaGrad and RMSProp scaled each parameter by its gradient
history.

\textbf{The idea.} Combine the two: track an exponential moving average of the
gradient (first moment) and of its square (second moment), correct both for
their initialisation at zero, and step by their ratio.

\textbf{The result.} An optimizer whose step size is invariant to rescaling the
gradient, that needs little tuning, and that worked well across the models of
the time. It became the default optimizer for neural networks.

\textbf{Why it is here.} It is the update of \cref{sec:adam}. The bias
correction --- the division by $1 - \beta^t$ --- is the detail most often
dropped in a hand implementation, and without it the first steps are far too
small.
\parencite{kingma2015}
\end{researchspotlight}

\section{Convolutional Layers, Briefly}
\label{sec:conv}

Every layer so far has been \emph{dense}: each unit sees every activation of the
previous layer. On images and signals that is wasteful.

\begin{defbox}{Convolutional layer}
In a \term{convolutional layer} each unit looks only at a \emph{window} --- a
small, contiguous region of its input --- and the \emph{same} weights, called a
\term{filter}, are slid across all windows. A layer usually has several
filters, each detecting one pattern wherever it occurs.
\end{defbox}

\begin{workedexample}{Classifying a heart signal}
A recording of a heartbeat is $100$ samples, $x_1,\dots,x_{100}$. A first layer
whose units look at windows of $20$ samples, moving $10$ samples at a time,
needs units for windows starting at $1, 11, \dots, 81$: nine units. Its parameter
count depends on how the weights are organised:

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
\toprule
layer type & parameters\\
\midrule
dense, $100\to9$ & $100\cdot9 + 9 = 909$\\
separate weights per window & $9\cdot(20+1) = 189$\\
one shared filter (convolutional) & $20 + 1 = 21$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A second convolutional layer might look at windows of five of the nine
activations, and a sigmoid unit at the end decide whether the heart is
abnormal.
\end{workedexample}

Fewer parameters mean faster computation, less data needed, and less
overfitting. Weight sharing also builds in an assumption that is usually true of
images and signals: a pattern means the same thing wherever it appears. The
sequence models of \cref{ch:ch18} start from the same observation about text.

\begin{researchspotlight}{LeCun et al.\ (1998) --- Gradient-based learning for document recognition}
\textbf{The problem.} Recognising handwritten characters needed hand-designed
feature extractors, which were brittle and expensive to build for each new task.

\textbf{The idea.} A network of convolutional layers with shared weights,
alternating with sub-sampling layers, trained end to end by back-propagation
directly from pixels --- LeNet-5. The convolutions learn the feature extractors
that had been designed by hand.

\textbf{The result.} State-of-the-art accuracy on handwritten digits, and a
deployed system that read a significant fraction of the cheques written in the
United States. Fourteen years later a much larger convolutional network, trained
on graphics processors with ReLU units, won the ImageNet competition by a wide
margin and began the modern era of deep learning \parencite{krizhevsky2012}.

\textbf{Why it is here.} It is \cref{sec:conv} at full scale, and the clearest
early demonstration of the key insight of \cref{ch:ch08}: features learned from
data beat features designed by hand, given enough data.
\parencite{lecun1998}
\end{researchspotlight}

\section*{Chapter Summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Chapter Summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Training a network is the same three steps as before: model, loss,
        minimisation --- \code{Sequential}, \code{compile}, \code{fit}. Binary
        cross-entropy is the logistic loss.
  \item The output activation is chosen from the range of the target: sigmoid,
        linear, ReLU, or softmax. Hidden layers use ReLU by default; it does not
        saturate for $z > 0$, where its slope is $1$ against the sigmoid's
        maximum of $0.25$.
  \item Softmax turns $N$ scores into $N$ probabilities; two-class softmax is the
        sigmoid of the score difference. Its cross-entropy loss $-\ln a_y$ has
        gradient $\va - \mathbf{e}_y$ with respect to the scores.
  \item The naive softmax overflows and loses precision. Subtract the maximum
        score and compute log-softmax directly; in Keras, output logits and pass
        \code{from\_logits=True}, or a confidently wrong example's loss is
        clipped at $16.12$.
  \item Multi-label problems use $N$ sigmoid outputs, not softmax.
  \item Back-propagation stores every value on the forward pass and multiplies
        local derivatives on the backward pass, computing all gradients in about
        the cost of one forward pass. For a dense layer,
        $d\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{A}_{\text{in}}^{\mathsf{T}}d\mathbf{Z}$ and
        $d\mathbf{Z}_{\text{in}} = (d\mathbf{Z}\,\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf{T}})\odot\gsig'$.
  \item Gradient checks against central differences catch implementation
        errors, but fail spuriously at ReLU's kink.
  \item Adam scales each parameter's step by its gradient history. On
        $w_1^2 + 10w_2^2$ gradient descent converges only for $\alpha < 0.1$;
        Adam converges for every $\alpha$ from $0.03$ to $3$.
  \item Convolutional layers share one filter across windows: $21$ parameters
        where a dense layer needs $909$.
\end{itemize}
