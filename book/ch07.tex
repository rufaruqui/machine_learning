\chapter{Overfitting and Regularization}
\label{ch:ch07}

\epigraph{With four parameters I can fit an elephant, and with five I can make
him wiggle his trunk.}{---\ attributed to John von Neumann}

\begin{chapterlearning}
After working through this chapter you will be able to:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Recognise underfitting and overfitting from a plot and from a pair of
        numbers --- training cost and test cost --- and say which is which.
  \item Explain why enlarging the hypothesis space can never raise the training
        cost, and why it can raise the test cost without limit.
  \item List the three standard remedies for overfitting and say what each
        costs.
  \item Write the regularized cost for linear and logistic regression and
        explain what $\lambda$ trades off, at both extremes.
  \item Derive the regularized gradient-descent update and read its first term
        as a shrinkage of every weight by $1 - \alpha\lambda/m$.
  \item Derive the closed-form ridge solution and show that the penalty makes it
        exist even when $\Xmat^{\mathsf{T}}\Xmat$ is singular.
  \item Explain why the bias $b$ is not penalised, and demonstrate what goes
        wrong if it is.
  \item Contrast L2 and L1 penalties through the constraint-region picture, and
        explain why only L1 produces exact zeros.
  \item Choose $\lambda$ on a logarithmic grid using held-out data, with the
        features scaled first.
\end{enumerate}
\end{chapterlearning}

\Cref{ch:ch05} ended with a model that passed exactly through all ten of its
training points and was plainly the worst description of the data in its table.
\Cref{ch:ch06} ended with another: perfectly separable data on which the
logistic coefficients grew for as long as gradient descent was allowed to run.
Both failures have the same cause, and this chapter names it, measures it, and
supplies the standard defence.

The defence is a single extra term in the cost function. It is the first
modification we make to a cost for a reason other than the fit to the data, and
it is worth noticing how little else changes: the model, the optimizer and the
decision rule all survive intact.

\begin{defbox}{Underfitting and overfitting}
A model \term{underfits} when it is too simple to represent the pattern in the
data. Its training cost is high, and its test cost is about as high: it does
badly everywhere. This is also called \term{high bias} --- the model is biased
towards a shape the data does not have.

A model \term{overfits} when it fits the training data, noise included, so
closely that it no longer describes new data. Its training cost is low and its
test cost much higher. This is also called \term{high variance} --- a different
training set would have produced a very different model.

The quantity we actually care about is the error on data the model has not
seen, its \term{generalization} error. The test cost estimates it; the training
cost does not.
\end{defbox}

\Cref{ch:ch10} makes \emph{bias} and \emph{variance} precise, and turns the two
numbers $J_{\text{train}}$ and $J_{\text{test}}$ into a diagnostic procedure.
Here we need only the qualitative picture: degree $1$ underfits, degree $2$ is
right, and degrees $8$ and $9$ overfit. Degrees $3$ to $7$ are interesting in
their own way --- slightly worse than the quadratic on test data, but not
disastrously so. Their extra flexibility is mostly unused.

\begin{keyinsight}{Read the gap, not either number alone}
A low training cost says the model can represent the training data; it says
nothing about new data. A high test cost says something is wrong, but not what.
The \emph{pair} is the diagnosis: both high is underfitting; training low and
test high is overfitting; both low is what we want. On T9 the degree-$9$ model's
training cost is the best in the table and its test cost thirty-six times worse
than the quadratic's.
\end{keyinsight}

\section{Why More Parameters Buy Training Accuracy}
\label{sec:nested}

The training column of the table falls monotonically. That is not a property of
T9; it is a theorem.

\begin{derivation}{Training cost cannot rise as the hypothesis space grows}
Let $\Hspace_d$ be the polynomials of degree at most $d$. Every polynomial of
degree $d$ is a polynomial of degree $d+1$ whose leading coefficient happens to
be zero, so
\[
  \Hspace_1 \subseteq \Hspace_2 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq \Hspace_9 .
\]
Let $f_d^\star$ minimise $J_{\text{train}}$ over $\Hspace_d$. Because
$f_d^\star \in \Hspace_{d+1}$, it is one of the candidates over which
$f_{d+1}^\star$ is the minimiser, so
\[
  J_{\text{train}}(f_{d+1}^\star) \;\le\; J_{\text{train}}(f_d^\star).
\]
Nothing in the argument mentions polynomials beyond the nesting. It holds for
any family of hypothesis spaces in which each contains the last: adding a
feature, adding a hidden unit to a network, deepening a tree. Training cost is a
non-increasing function of capacity.
\end{derivation}

The argument says nothing at all about $J_{\text{test}}$, and nothing could: the
minimiser over $\Hspace_{d+1}$ is chosen to fit the training points, and the
extra freedom is spent wherever it reduces training error most. With ten
training points and ten parameters there is exactly one interpolating
polynomial, and the freedom is spent bending it through the noise.

The last column of the table shows how. The coefficients of the degree-$9$ fit
reach $4.3$ million, on data whose values never exceed $36$. Large coefficients
of opposite sign cancel at the training points --- where they were fitted ---
and fail to cancel between them. This is the signature of overfitting that
regularization targets directly: \emph{an overfitted model is a model with very
large weights}.

\begin{pitfall}{More data points do not mean more parameters are safe}
It is tempting to read the T9 failure as ``degree $9$ is too high'' and conclude
that some fixed degree is safe. What matters is the ratio of parameters to
examples, and the noise level. With a thousand training points the degree-$9$
fit would be close to the quadratic, because the noise would average out. With
ten, even degree $3$ is spending some freedom on noise. There is no degree that
is right independently of the data.
\end{pitfall}

\section{Addressing Overfitting}
\label{sec:remedies}

There are three standard remedies, and they act on different parts of the
learning problem of \cref{sec:four-parts}.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Collect more training data.} The data part. The interpolating
        curve has nothing left to bend through once there are many more points
        than parameters. This is the most reliable remedy, and often the most
        expensive: labels cost money and time. \Cref{ch:ch10}'s learning curves
        tell you in advance whether it will help, and \cref{ch:ch11} discusses
        cheaper ways to get more data.
  \item \textbf{Use fewer features.} The hypothesis-space part. Choose a lower
        degree, or select a subset of the original features by hand or by an
        algorithm. This works, but it is a blunt instrument: a discarded
        feature contributes nothing, even if it carried a little real signal.
  \item \textbf{Regularize.} The cost part. Keep every feature, but add a term
        to the cost that penalises large weights, so the optimizer prefers small
        ones. The model can still use all its features; it simply has to pay to
        lean heavily on any of them.
\end{enumerate}

The third remedy is the subject of the rest of the chapter, because it is
continuous where the second is discrete --- one dial, $\lambda$, instead of an
on/off switch per feature --- and because it is the one that carries over
unchanged to logistic regression and to the networks of \cref{ch:ch08}.

\section{The Regularized Cost}
\label{sec:regularized-cost}

\begin{defbox}{Regularized cost (L2, ridge)}
For a model with weights $\vw \in \Real{n}$ and bias $b$, the
\term{regularized} cost is
\[
  J(\vw,b) \;=\;
  \underbrace{\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(\fvwb(\xii{i}) - \yii{i}\bigr)^2}_{\text{fit to the data}}
  \;+\;
  \underbrace{\frac{\lambda}{2m}\sum_{j=1}^{n} w_j^2}_{\text{penalty on the weights}} ,
\]
where $\lambda \ge 0$ is the \term{regularization parameter}. The penalty sums
over the weights only --- not over $b$ (\cref{sec:why-not-b}). It is called an
\term{L2 penalty}, or \term{ridge} penalty, because $\sum_j w_j^2 =
\|\vw\|_2^2$; in neural networks the same term is called \term{weight decay}.
\end{defbox}

The factor $\frac{1}{2m}$ in front of the penalty matches the factor in front of
the data term. The $\frac12$ cancels on differentiation, as before; the
$\frac1m$ means that $\lambda$ trades the penalty against the \emph{average}
squared error, so the same $\lambda$ means roughly the same thing for a hundred
examples as for a million.

The two extremes make the trade-off plain.

\begin{itemize}
  \item $\lambda = 0$: no penalty, and we are back to ordinary least squares and
        the degree-$9$ curve of \cref{fig:three-fits}.
  \item $\lambda \to \infty$: any non-zero weight costs an unbounded amount, so
        $\vw \to \mathbf{0}$ and $f(\vx) \to b$, a horizontal line at the mean
        of $y$ --- the most underfitted model there is.
\end{itemize}

Between the two, $\lambda$ sets how much training fit the model must give up to
earn a reduction in $\|\vw\|$.

\section{Regularized Linear Regression}
\label{sec:reg-linreg}

\subsection{Gradient descent}

The penalty changes the gradient with respect to each weight and leaves the
gradient with respect to $b$ alone.

\begin{derivation}{The regularized update as shrinkage}
Differentiate the regularized cost. The data term gives exactly the gradient of
\cref{sec:gd-multi}; the penalty $\frac{\lambda}{2m}\sum_k w_k^2$ contributes
$\frac{\lambda}{m}w_j$ to the $j$-th partial derivative and nothing to the
partial with respect to $b$:
\[
  \frac{\partial J}{\partial w_j}
    = \frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(\fvwb(\xii{i})-\yii{i}\bigr)\xjj{i}{j}
      + \frac{\lambda}{m}w_j ,
  \qquad
  \frac{\partial J}{\partial b}
    = \frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(\fvwb(\xii{i})-\yii{i}\bigr).
\]
Substitute into the update $w_j \leftarrow w_j - \alpha\,\partial J/\partial w_j$
and collect the two terms in $w_j$:
\[
  w_j \;\leftarrow\;
  \underbrace{\Bigl(1 - \alpha\frac{\lambda}{m}\Bigr) w_j}_{\text{shrink}}
  \;-\;
  \underbrace{\alpha\,\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(\fvwb(\xii{i})-\yii{i}\bigr)\xjj{i}{j}}_{\text{the unregularized step}} .
\]
The second term is the familiar update. The first multiplies every weight by a
factor slightly less than one before the step is taken. With $\alpha = 0.01$,
$\lambda = 1$ and $m = 10$, the factor is $1 - 0.001 = 0.9990$: each iteration
shrinks every weight by $0.1\%$, then lets the data push it back where the data
insists.

The update for $b$ is unchanged.
\end{derivation}

This reading explains the name \emph{weight decay}: left alone, with no data
pressure, every weight would decay geometrically to zero, by a factor of
$1-\alpha\lambda/m$ per step. A weight survives only if the data keeps paying
for it. For the result to be a shrinkage rather than an oscillation the factor
must lie in $(0,1)$, which requires $\alpha\lambda < m$; in practice it is
always very close to one.

Running this update on T9 with all nine powers, $\lambda = 1$ and
$\alpha = 0.05$, for $200{,}000$ iterations from the origin, reproduces the
closed-form solution of the next subsection to within $3\times10^{-14}$ in every
weight. The two routes find the same minimiser, as they must: the regularized
cost is a convex quadratic, so it has exactly one.

\subsection{The closed form}

The normal equation of \cref{sec:normal-equation} extends directly.

\begin{derivation}{Ridge regression in closed form}
Absorb $b$ into the weights as in \cref{ch:ch04}: prepend a column of ones to
the design matrix, $\tilde\Xmat = [\mathbf{1}\ \Xmat]$, and write
$\boldsymbol\theta = (b, w_1, \dots, w_n)$. Let $I'$ be the
$(n+1)\times(n+1)$ identity with its top-left entry set to zero, so that
$\boldsymbol\theta^{\mathsf{T}}I'\boldsymbol\theta = \sum_{j\ge1}w_j^2$ excludes
$b$. The cost is
\[
  J(\boldsymbol\theta) = \frac{1}{2m}\|\tilde\Xmat\boldsymbol\theta - \vy\|^2
                       + \frac{\lambda}{2m}\boldsymbol\theta^{\mathsf{T}}I'\boldsymbol\theta .
\]
Its gradient is
$\frac1m\bigl(\tilde\Xmat^{\mathsf{T}}(\tilde\Xmat\boldsymbol\theta - \vy)
+ \lambda I'\boldsymbol\theta\bigr)$; setting it to zero gives
\[
  \boxed{\;\bigl(\tilde\Xmat^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde\Xmat + \lambda I'\bigr)\boldsymbol\theta
  = \tilde\Xmat^{\mathsf{T}}\vy\;}
\]
--- the normal equations with $\lambda$ added to every diagonal entry except
the one belonging to $b$.
\end{derivation}

The added diagonal does more than change the answer: it guarantees there is one.
\Cref{sec:singular} showed that $\tilde\Xmat^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde\Xmat$ is singular
when features are duplicated or linearly dependent, so the unregularized normal
equations have infinitely many solutions. For any $\lambda > 0$ the matrix
$\tilde\Xmat^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde\Xmat + \lambda I'$ is invertible provided the
column of ones is not itself the problem, because
$\boldsymbol\theta^{\mathsf{T}}(\tilde\Xmat^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde\Xmat + \lambda
I')\boldsymbol\theta = \|\tilde\Xmat\boldsymbol\theta\|^2 + \lambda\sum_{j\ge1}w_j^2$,
which is zero only if every $w_j = 0$ and then $b\cdot\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{0}$,
so $b = 0$ too.

\begin{workedexample}{A duplicated feature, resolved}
Take the first standardised power of T9 and include it twice, so the design
matrix has three columns of which two are identical. The unregularized
$\tilde\Xmat^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde\Xmat$ has rank $2$ out of $3$: any split
$w_1 + w_2 = c$ fits equally well, and the normal equations cannot choose.

With $\lambda = 1$ the system has the unique solution $b = 14.2400$,
$w_1 = w_2 = 4.9206$. The penalty breaks the tie in the only symmetric way:
for a fixed sum $w_1 + w_2$, the sum of squares $w_1^2 + w_2^2$ is smallest
when the two are equal. As a check, two equal copies each carrying $w$ pay
$\lambda\cdot 2w^2 = \tfrac{\lambda}{2}(2w)^2$, which is a single copy carrying
$2w$ under penalty $\lambda/2$; fitting one copy with $\lambda = 0.5$ gives
$9.8412$, and half of that is $4.9206$.
\end{workedexample}

\begin{researchspotlight}{Hoerl \& Kennard (1970) --- Ridge regression}
\textbf{The problem.} In regression on many correlated predictors, least-squares
coefficients are unstable: small changes in the data swing them wildly and flip
their signs, and $\Xmat^{\mathsf{T}}\Xmat$ is close to singular. The estimates
are unbiased, but their variance is enormous.

\textbf{The idea.} Add a small constant $k$ to the diagonal of
$\Xmat^{\mathsf{T}}\Xmat$ before inverting. Plot every coefficient against $k$
--- the \emph{ridge trace} --- and choose the smallest $k$ at which they
stabilise. The name echoes Hoerl's earlier \emph{ridge analysis} of response
surfaces.

\textbf{The result.} They proved that for some $k > 0$ the ridge estimator has
strictly smaller mean squared error than least squares: it accepts a little bias
in exchange for a larger reduction in variance. The existence of such a $k$ is
guaranteed whatever the true coefficients are.

\textbf{Why it is here.} It is the closed form of \cref{sec:reg-linreg}, with
$k$ playing the role of $\lambda$, and its central claim --- that a deliberately
biased estimator can beat the best unbiased one --- is the bias/variance
trade-off that \cref{ch:ch10} develops into a diagnostic.
\parencite{hoerl1970}
\end{researchspotlight}

\subsection{Why the bias is not penalised}
\label{sec:why-not-b}

The penalty sums from $j = 1$, leaving $b$ out, and this is not a convenience.
The weights control how sharply the model responds to changes in the features
--- that is what overfitting abuses. The bias controls only the overall level of
the predictions, which a model needs to get right whatever its complexity.
Penalising it makes the fit depend on an arbitrary choice of origin for $y$.

\begin{workedexample}{Shifting the targets}
Fit the first two standardised powers of T9 with $\lambda = 10$, twice: once on
the targets as given and once with $100$ added to every target, which should
change nothing except the level of every prediction. Report the prediction at
$x = 5.5$ with the shift subtracted back off.

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
 & shift & $b$ & $f(5.5) - \text{shift}$\\
\midrule
$b$ not penalised & $0$   & $14.2400$  & $13.2737$\\
                  & $100$ & $114.2400$ & $13.2737$\\
\midrule
$b$ penalised     & $0$   & $7.1200$   & $6.1537$\\
                  & $100$ & $57.1200$  & $-43.8463$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Leave $b$ unpenalised and the shift simply passes through to $b$; the prediction
is unchanged. Penalise it and $b$ is pulled towards zero by an amount that
depends on how far the targets happen to sit from zero: with $m = 10$ and
$\lambda = 10$ it becomes $\sum_i \yii{i}/(m+\lambda)$, exactly half the mean.
A model whose predictions change by $50$ because the targets were recorded in
different units of origin has learned nothing reliable. (The weights are
identical in all four rows because standardised features have mean zero, which
decouples $b$ from $\vw$ entirely.)
\end{workedexample}

\section{Regularized Logistic Regression}
\label{sec:reg-logistic}

Nothing in the penalty depends on the data term, so it attaches to the logistic
cost of \cref{sec:logistic-loss} unchanged.

\begin{defbox}{Regularized logistic cost}
\[
  J(\vw,b) = -\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Bigl[\yii{i}\ln f^{(i)}
              + \bigl(1-\yii{i}\bigr)\ln\bigl(1-f^{(i)}\bigr)\Bigr]
           + \frac{\lambda}{2m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_j^2 ,
  \qquad f^{(i)} = \gsig(\vw\cdot\xii{i} + b).
\]
Its gradient is that of \cref{sec:gd-logistic} with $\frac{\lambda}{m}w_j$ added
to each weight component, so the update is again a shrinkage by
$1-\alpha\lambda/m$ followed by the unregularized step.
\end{defbox}

The penalty also adds $\frac{\lambda}{m}I'$ to the Hessian of
\cref{sec:logistic-convexity}. That matrix was positive semi-definite; adding a
positive multiple of the identity on the weight block makes the cost strictly
convex in $\vw$ whatever the data, which is the cure for the pathology that
closed \cref{ch:ch06}.

\begin{workedexample}{Separable data now has a finite fit}
Take T7 with the $2$~cm malignancy removed --- seven points separated perfectly
by any threshold between $5$ and $6$~cm --- for which \cref{ch:ch06} showed the unregularized coefficients
growing without bound. With the penalty, Newton's method converges to a finite
minimiser for every $\lambda > 0$:

$0.01$  & $-31.0923$ & $5.6531$ & $5.5000$ & $0.016496$\\
$0.1$   & $-14.7120$ & $2.6749$ & $5.5000$ & $0.072081$\\

Small $\lambda$ gives a steep sigmoid with the boundary midway between the

\section{L2 and L1 Penalties}
\label{sec:l1-l2}

The squared penalty is not the only choice. Replace $w_j^2$ by $|w_j|$ and the
behaviour changes qualitatively.

\begin{defbox}{The L1 penalty (lasso)}
\[
  J(\vw,b) = \frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(\fvwb(\xii{i})-\yii{i}\bigr)^2
           + \frac{\lambda}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|w_j| .
\]
The penalty is $\frac{\lambda}{m}\|\vw\|_1$. Linear regression with this penalty
is called the \term{lasso}, for ``least absolute shrinkage and selection
operator''.
\end{defbox}

The difference is best seen through an equivalent formulation. Minimising the
data cost plus $\lambda$ times a penalty is, for a suitable budget $t$ that
depends on $\lambda$, the same as minimising the data cost alone subject to the
penalty not exceeding $t$. For L2 the feasible region $\|\vw\|_2 \le t$ is a
disc; for L1, $\|\vw\|_1 \le t$ is a diamond with corners on the axes.

\begin{pitfall}{Sparse is not the same as true}
The lasso kept $x^2$ and $x^3$, but the curve that generated T9 has $x$ and
$x^2$. The two sets of powers are highly correlated over $[1,10]$, and among
correlated features the lasso tends to pick one somewhat arbitrarily and zero
the rest. Treat the features it keeps as a compact description that predicts
well, not as a discovery about which variables matter.
\end{pitfall}

The practical guidance follows from the picture. Use L2 by default: it is
differentiable everywhere, has a closed form for linear models, and handles
groups of correlated features by sharing the weight among them. Use L1 when you
want a model that uses few features --- for interpretability, or because each
feature is expensive to measure at prediction time. The \term{elastic net},
which adds both penalties, is a common compromise.

\begin{researchspotlight}{Tibshirani (1996) --- The lasso}
\textbf{The problem.} Ridge regression improves prediction but keeps every
predictor, which leaves a model that is hard to interpret. Subset selection
gives interpretable models but is discrete, unstable, and computationally
explosive.

\textbf{The idea.} Minimise the residual sum of squares subject to
$\sum_j|w_j| \le t$. The constraint is convex, so the problem is tractable; and
because the constraint region has corners, the solution has some coefficients
exactly zero.

\textbf{The result.} A single method that both shrinks and selects. As $t$
decreases, coefficients leave the model one by one, tracing a piecewise-linear
path; the paper's diamond-versus-disc figure is the ancestor of
\cref{fig:l1-l2}.

\textbf{Why it is here.} It is the L1 penalty of \cref{sec:l1-l2}. It also
opened a large field --- compressed sensing and sparse modelling more broadly
--- built on the observation that L1 is the closest convex relative of simply
counting the non-zero coefficients.
\parencite{tibshirani1996}
\end{researchspotlight}

\section{Choosing \texorpdfstring{$\lambda$}{lambda}}
\label{sec:choose-lambda}

The sweep of \cref{sec:regularized-cost} is the procedure, with two
refinements.

\textbf{Search on a logarithmic grid.} The useful values of $\lambda$ spanned
seven orders of magnitude on T9, and would on most problems. A grid that roughly
doubles or triples at each step --- $0.01, 0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 1, 3, 10, \dots$ ---
covers that range in a dozen fits, the same $\times3$ ladder used for the
learning rate in \cref{sec:alpha-ladder}.

\textbf{Scale the features first.} The penalty $\sum_j w_j^2$ treats every
weight alike, but the size of a weight depends on the units of its feature. A
feature measured in centimetres needs a weight one hundred times smaller than
the same feature in metres to have the same effect, and so pays ten thousand
times less penalty.

\begin{pitfall}{Regularizing unscaled features}
Fit T9 with the two raw features $x$ and $x^2$ and $\lambda = 10$, first with $x$
read as metres and then, identically, as centimetres:

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
unit of $x$ & $(w_1, w_2)$ & $J_{\text{train}}$ & $f(5.5\text{ m})$\\
\midrule
metres      & $(-0.4201,\ 0.3699)$   & $1.5463$ & $11.1886$\\
centimetres & $(-0.0151,\ 0.000046)$ & $1.2997$ & $10.4090$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Same data, same model, same $\lambda$, different fits. In centimetres the
weights are so small that the penalty is negligible and the fit is the
unregularized quadratic ($J_{\text{train}} = 1.2997$, as in
\cref{sec:overfitting}); in metres the penalty bites. A value of $\lambda$ has
no meaning until the features are on a common scale --- which is one more reason
the standardisation of \cref{sec:feature-scaling} comes before everything else.
\end{pitfall}

\textbf{Choose on held-out data --- but not on the test set.} The sweep above
reported test cost so that its shape would be visible, and choosing $\lambda$ by
reading that column would give about $0.005$. But a $\lambda$ chosen because it
minimises test cost has been fitted to the test set, and the test cost at that
$\lambda$ is no longer an honest estimate of performance on new data: it is the
best of nine tries. The cure is a third set, the \emph{validation} set, held out
from training and used to choose $\lambda$, leaving the test set untouched until
the very end. \Cref{ch:ch10} builds that procedure, and its more economical
relative, cross-validation.

\begin{keyinsight}{Regularization buys the right to over-parameterize}
Before this chapter the safe course was to choose a small model. Regularization
inverts the advice: choose a model flexible enough to capture whatever pattern
is there, and let the penalty, tuned on held-out data, decide how much of that
flexibility the data can support. On T9, nine features and a penalty matched
the true-degree model to within $0.03$ in test cost, without anyone telling the
model the true degree. The same strategy, at scale, is how large neural networks
are trained.
\end{keyinsight}

\section*{Chapter Summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Chapter Summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item A model \emph{underfits} when training and test cost are both high, and
        \emph{overfits} when training cost is low and test cost much higher. On
        T9 the straight line underfits ($6.99$, $5.82$), the quadratic is right
        ($1.30$, $0.48$), and the degree-$9$ interpolant overfits ($0.00$,
        $17.46$).
  \item Training cost cannot rise as nested hypothesis spaces grow; test cost can
        rise without limit. Overfitted models show very large weights that
        cancel at the training points --- $4.3$ million on T9.
  \item The remedies are more data, fewer features, and regularization. The
        third is continuous, keeps every feature, and carries over unchanged to
        logistic regression and neural networks.
  \item The L2 penalty $\frac{\lambda}{2m}\sum_j w_j^2$ trades training fit for
        smaller weights. $\lambda=0$ is least squares; $\lambda\to\infty$ is the
        horizontal line at $\bar y$. On T9, $\lambda = 10^{-4}$ alone cuts the
        test cost from $17.46$ to $0.58$.
  \item The regularized update is a shrinkage of every weight by
        $1-\alpha\lambda/m$ followed by the ordinary step. The closed form adds
        $\lambda$ to the diagonal of the normal equations and is solvable even
        when $\Xmat^{\mathsf{T}}\Xmat$ is singular.
  \item The bias is not penalised: penalising it makes predictions depend on the
        origin of $y$, by $50$ units in the shifted-target example.
  \item Regularized logistic regression is strictly convex in $\vw$ and has a
        finite minimiser even on separable data.
  \item L1 penalties produce exact zeros because the diamond's corners lie on
        the axes; L2 shrinks without eliminating. The lasso kept two of T9's
        nine powers.
  \item Choose $\lambda$ on a logarithmic grid, after scaling the features, using
        a validation set rather than the test set.
\end{itemize}

\exercisesection{Analytical Problems}

\ap Prove that if $\Hspace_1 \subseteq \Hspace_2$, the minimum training cost over
$\Hspace_2$ is at most that over $\Hspace_1$. Explain why no corresponding
statement holds for the test cost.

\ap Derive the regularized gradient-descent update for linear regression and
show that it can be written as a shrinkage step followed by the unregularized
step. Under what condition on $\alpha$, $\lambda$ and $m$ is the shrinkage
factor in $(0,1)$?

\ap Derive the closed-form ridge solution
$(\tilde\Xmat^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde\Xmat + \lambda I')\boldsymbol\theta =
\tilde\Xmat^{\mathsf{T}}\vy$, and prove that the matrix on the left is invertible
for every $\lambda > 0$.

\ap For one feature, centred so that $\sum_i \xii{i} = 0$, show that the ridge
weight is $w = \sum_i\xii{i}\yii{i}/(\sum_i(\xii{i})^2 + \lambda)$ and that
$b = \bar y$. Hence show that $|w|$ decreases monotonically in $\lambda$.

\ap Suppose the bias is penalised, the feature is centred and $\lambda > 0$.
Show that $b = \sum_i\yii{i}/(m+\lambda)$, and use this to explain the
shifted-target table of \cref{sec:why-not-b}.

\ap Write the regularized logistic cost and its gradient, and show that its
Hessian is positive definite in the weights for every $\lambda > 0$, even when
the data is linearly separable.

\ap For the one-dimensional lasso cost
$\frac12(w - a)^2 + \mu|w|$ with $\mu > 0$, show that the minimiser is
$w^\star = \operatorname{sign}(a)\max(|a| - \mu, 0)$. Explain how this
\emph{soft-thresholding} produces exact zeros, and why the ridge analogue
$\frac12(w-a)^2 + \frac{\mu}{2}w^2$ never does.

\exercisesection{Self-Test}

\qz A model with low training cost and high test cost is: (a)~underfitting;
(b)~overfitting; (c)~well fitted; (d)~unregularized but correct.

\qz As $\lambda \to \infty$ in ridge regression, the fitted function tends to:
(a)~the least-squares fit; (b)~zero everywhere; (c)~the constant $\bar y$;
(d)~the interpolating polynomial.

\qz The regularized update multiplies each weight by: (a)~$1 + \alpha\lambda/m$;
(b)~$1 - \alpha\lambda/m$; (c)~$\lambda$; (d)~$1 - \alpha/m$.

\qz The bias term is not regularized because: (a)~it has no gradient;
(b)~penalising it makes predictions depend on the origin of $y$; (c)~it is
always zero; (d)~it cannot overfit by convention only.

\qz Adding $\lambda > 0$ to the diagonal of the normal equations: (a)~can make
them singular; (b)~guarantees a unique solution; (c)~has no effect on the
solution; (d)~removes the need for features.

\qz Which penalty can set weights exactly to zero? (a)~L2; (b)~L1; (c)~both;
(d)~neither.

\qz The test cost plotted against $\lambda$ is typically: (a)~increasing;
(b)~decreasing; (c)~U-shaped; (d)~constant.

\qz Choosing $\lambda$ by minimising the test cost: (a)~is the correct
procedure; (b)~makes the reported test cost optimistic; (c)~is impossible;
(d)~guarantees the best model.

\qz Regularization applied to features of very different scales: (a)~is
unaffected by the scales; (b)~penalises small-valued features less;
(c)~penalises features in large units less; (d)~requires L1.

regression must be applied to standardised predictors, and §6.2.2 the lasso,

which L1 and L2 are the two important members.
