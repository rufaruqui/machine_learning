\chapter{Recommender Systems}
\label{ch:ch15}

\epigraph{People who liked this also liked \dots}{---\ every online shop}

\begin{chapterlearning}
After working through this chapter you will be able to:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Describe a recommendation problem as a ratings matrix with missing
        entries, in standard notation.
  \item Predict ratings from known item features with one linear model per user.
  \item Derive and run collaborative filtering, which learns item features and
        user preferences together from the ratings alone.
  \item Adapt it to binary feedback such as likes and clicks.
  \item Use mean normalization to give new users sensible predictions.
  \item Find related items from learned features.
  \item Compare collaborative and content-based filtering, and design a
        two-tower network and a retrieval-and-ranking pipeline for a large
        catalogue.
  \item Evaluate a recommender honestly, and say what it should and should not
        optimise.
\end{enumerate}
\end{chapterlearning}

A recommender system decides what to show a user next: which product, which
song, which video, which restaurant dish. It is arguably the most commercially
important application of machine learning, because so much of what people buy,
watch and read online is chosen for them by one. It is also a good place to see
the ideas of the book combine: linear regression, regularization, gradient
descent, learned features and neural networks all appear in this one chapter.

\section{The Ratings Matrix}
\label{sec:ratings}

\begin{defbox}{Dataset R54}
A food-delivery app records how four users rated five dishes, from $0$ to $5$
stars. A dash means the user has not rated the dish.

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}
\toprule
dish & Alice & Bob & Carol & Dave\\
\midrule
Chicken Curry   & $5$ & $5$ & $0$ & $0$\\
Chilli Noodles  & $5$ & --- & --- & $0$\\
Pepper Soup     & --- & $4$ & $0$ & ---\\
Ginger Cake     & $0$ & $0$ & $5$ & $4$\\
Mango Ice Cream & $0$ & $0$ & $5$ & ---\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{defbox}

\begin{defbox}{Recommender notation}
$n_u$ is the number of users and $n_m$ the number of items ($n_u = 4$, $n_m = 5$
in R54). $r(i,j) = 1$ if user $j$ has rated item $i$, and then $y^{(i,j)}$ is
the rating. The task is to predict $y^{(i,j)}$ wherever $r(i,j) = 0$, and to
recommend each user the unrated items with the highest predictions.
\end{defbox}

Even by eye, Alice and Bob like spicy food and dislike sweets, and Carol and Dave
the reverse, so Alice should probably be offered the Pepper Soup. Real matrices
are enormous and almost empty --- millions of users, millions of items, and each
user rating a tiny fraction --- but the structure of the problem is the same.

\section{Content-Based Prediction with Item Features}
\label{sec:content-based}

Suppose each dish has been described by two features: how spicy it is, $x_1$,
and how sweet, $x_2$.

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
dish & $x_1$ spicy & $x_2$ sweet\\
\midrule
Chicken Curry   & $0.90$ & $0$\\
Chilli Noodles  & $1.00$ & $0.01$\\
Pepper Soup     & $0.99$ & $0$\\
Ginger Cake     & $0.10$ & $1.00$\\
Mango Ice Cream & $0$    & $0.90$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Then each user can be modelled by linear regression on those features.

\begin{defbox}{Content-based prediction}
Predict user $j$'s rating of item $i$ as
\[
  \hat y^{(i,j)} = \vw^{(j)}\cdot\vx^{(i)} + b^{(j)},
\]
with parameters $\vw^{(j)}, b^{(j)}$ for each user, fitted by minimising
\[
  J\bigl(\vw^{(j)}, b^{(j)}\bigr) = \frac12\sum_{i:\,r(i,j)=1}\bigl(\vw^{(j)}\cdot\vx^{(i)} + b^{(j)} - y^{(i,j)}\bigr)^2
    + \frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigl(w^{(j)}_k\bigr)^2 .
\]
To learn all users at once, sum this cost over $j$.
\end{defbox}

The $\frac1m$ of earlier chapters is dropped by convention here, because each
user has a different number of ratings; it would only rescale $\lambda$.

\begin{workedexample}{Predicting Alice's rating}
If Alice's parameters were $\vw^{(1)} = (5,\ 0)$ and $b^{(1)} = 0$ --- loving
spice, indifferent to sweetness --- her predicted rating of Pepper Soup,
$\vx^{(3)} = (0.99,\ 0)$, would be $5(0.99) + 0 = 4.95$.

Actually fitting each user with $\lambda = 1$ gives Alice
$\vw = (1.66,\ -1.75)$, $b = 2.506$, and a predicted Pepper Soup rating of
$4.15$; Dave gets $\vw = (-1.06,\ 1.24)$ and a predicted Mango Ice Cream rating
of $2.74$. With only three ratings per user the penalty shrinks the fits
noticeably.
\end{workedexample}

The prediction is a dot product, which has a useful geometric reading:
$\vw^{(j)}\cdot\vx^{(i)}$ is large when the item's feature vector points the way
the user's preference vector points. Alice's vector points towards spicy and away
from sweet; Carol's the opposite.

\begin{pitfall}{Who scores the features?}
Content-based prediction needs every item described by hand. For five dishes and
two qualities that is easy; for millions of items, and for qualities nobody
thought to name, it is impossible. The next section removes the requirement.
\end{pitfall}

\section{Collaborative Filtering}
\label{sec:collab}

Turn the problem around. If we knew every user's $\vw^{(j)}$ and $b^{(j)}$, we
could \emph{learn} each item's features from the ratings it received, by the
same least squares:
\[
  \min_{\vx^{(i)}}\ \frac12\sum_{j:\,r(i,j)=1}\bigl(\vw^{(j)}\cdot\vx^{(i)} + b^{(j)} - y^{(i,j)}\bigr)^2
    + \frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigl(x^{(i)}_k\bigr)^2 .
\]
This works only because many users rated each item: their ratings, together,
say what the item is like. Neither the features nor the preferences are known in
practice, so learn both at once.

\begin{defbox}{Collaborative filtering}
Learn item features $\vx^{(1)},\dots,\vx^{(n_m)}$ and user parameters
$\vw^{(1)},b^{(1)},\dots,\vw^{(n_u)},b^{(n_u)}$ by minimising
\[
  J = \frac12\!\!\sum_{(i,j):\,r(i,j)=1}\!\!\bigl(\vw^{(j)}\cdot\vx^{(i)} + b^{(j)} - y^{(i,j)}\bigr)^2
    + \frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n_u}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigl(w^{(j)}_k\bigr)^2
    + \frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n_m}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigl(x^{(i)}_k\bigr)^2 .
\]
The number of features $n$ is a hyperparameter. The method is
\term{collaborative} because every user's ratings help learn every item's
features, which in turn help predict every other user's ratings.
\end{defbox}

\begin{derivation}{The collaborative-filtering gradient}
Write $e^{(i,j)} = \vw^{(j)}\cdot\vx^{(i)} + b^{(j)} - y^{(i,j)}$ for a rated pair.
A weight $w^{(j)}_k$ appears in the error of every item user $j$ rated, with
coefficient $x^{(i)}_k$, and in one penalty term:
\[
  \frac{\partial J}{\partial w^{(j)}_k} = \sum_{i:\,r(i,j)=1} e^{(i,j)}\,x^{(i)}_k + \lambda\,w^{(j)}_k ,
  \qquad
  \frac{\partial J}{\partial b^{(j)}} = \sum_{i:\,r(i,j)=1} e^{(i,j)} .
\]
By symmetry, a feature $x^{(i)}_k$ appears in the error of every user who rated
item $i$, with coefficient $w^{(j)}_k$:
\[
  \frac{\partial J}{\partial x^{(i)}_k} = \sum_{j:\,r(i,j)=1} e^{(i,j)}\,w^{(j)}_k + \lambda\,x^{(i)}_k .
\]
In matrix form, with $\mathbf{X}$ the $n_m\times n$ matrix of item features,
$\mathbf{W}$ the $n_u\times n$ matrix of user weights, $\mathbf{R}$ the
indicator matrix and $\odot$ the entrywise product,
\[
  \mathbf{E} = \bigl(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf T} + \mathbf{1}\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf T} - \mathbf{Y}\bigr)\odot\mathbf{R},
  \qquad
  \frac{\partial J}{\partial\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{E}\mathbf{W} + \lambda\mathbf{X},
  \qquad
  \frac{\partial J}{\partial\mathbf{W}} = \mathbf{E}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{W} .
\]
Multiplying by $\mathbf{R}$ zeroes the unrated entries, so they contribute
nothing. Gradient descent updates all three blocks simultaneously.
\end{derivation}

\begin{workedexample}{Two gradient steps by hand}
Use one feature ($n = 1$), start from $\vx^{(i)} = 1$ for every dish,
$w^{(j)} = 1$ and $b^{(j)} = 0$ for every user, with $\lambda = 0$ and
$\alpha = 0.1$. Every prediction is then $1$, and the cost is $J = 53$.

\textbf{Step 1.} For Chicken Curry the errors on its four ratings are
$1-5,\ 1-5,\ 1-0,\ 1-0 = (-4,\ -4,\ 1,\ 1)$, so
$\partial J/\partial x^{(1)} = \sum_j e^{(1,j)}w^{(j)} = -6$. All five feature
gradients are $(-6,\ -3,\ -2,\ -5,\ -2)$, and the user gradients for $w$ are
$(-6,\ -5,\ -6,\ -1)$ --- equal, at this step, to those for $b$, because every
$x^{(i)}$ is $1$. After the update,
\[
  \vx = (1.6,\ 1.3,\ 1.2,\ 1.5,\ 1.2),\quad
  \vw = (1.6,\ 1.5,\ 1.6,\ 1.1),\quad
  \mathbf{b} = (0.6,\ 0.5,\ 0.6,\ 0.1).
\]
\textbf{Step 2.} The cost is now $40.8102$. The new gradients overshoot in places
--- the feature gradient for Mango Ice Cream is $+3.514$ --- and the update gives
\[
  \vx = (1.4992,\ 1.5029,\ 1.0518,\ 1.1750,\ 0.8486),\quad
  \vw = (1.4436,\ 1.3515,\ 1.3896,\ 0.9410),
\]
with cost $39.3652$. One feature cannot separate the spicy-lovers from the
sweet-lovers; the next run uses two.
\end{workedexample}

With $n = 2$, $\lambda = 0.1$, $\alpha = 0.01$ and a small random start, the
cost falls from $79.61$ to $11.17$ in $40$ steps, to $1.37$ in $80$, and to
$1.060$ after $2000$. The learned model fills in the matrix:

\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{\qquad}lr@{}}
\toprule
missing rating & prediction & missing rating & prediction\\
\midrule
Bob, Chilli Noodles   & $4.47$  & Dave, Pepper Soup     & $0.30$\\
Carol, Chilli Noodles & $-0.19$ & Dave, Mango Ice Cream & $3.94$\\
Alice, Pepper Soup    & $4.62$  &                       & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Recommend the Pepper Soup to Alice and the Mango Ice Cream to Dave. The observed

\begin{pitfall}{Learned features have no fixed meaning}
The learned axes are not ``spicy'' and ``sweet''. If $\mathbf{Q}$ is any
invertible $n\times n$ matrix, replacing $\mathbf{X}$ by $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Q}$
and $\mathbf{W}$ by $\mathbf{W}\mathbf{Q}^{-\mathsf T}$ leaves every prediction
$\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf T}$ unchanged. Only quantities invariant to such
changes --- predictions, and distances when $\mathbf{Q}$ is a rotation ---
mean anything.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{researchspotlight}{Koren, Bell \& Volinsky (2009) --- Matrix factorization for recommender systems}
\textbf{The problem.} The Netflix Prize offered a million dollars for a $10\%$
improvement in predicting movie ratings, on a matrix of $100$ million ratings
from half a million users of $17{,}770$ films.

\textbf{The idea.} Model each rating as a user vector dotted with an item vector,
plus user and item biases, learned by stochastic gradient descent or alternating
least squares with L2 regularization --- collaborative filtering as matrix
factorization --- and extend the model with implicit feedback and time-varying
effects.

\textbf{The result.} Latent-factor models were the backbone of the winning
entries, which blended hundreds of models. The paper, by members of the winning
team, became the standard reference for the method.

\textbf{Why it is here.} It is \cref{sec:collab}, with the explicit item and user
biases that this chapter folds into $b^{(j)}$ and the mean normalization of
\cref{sec:mean-norm}.
\parencite{koren2009}
\end{researchspotlight}

\section{Binary Labels}
\label{sec:binary-cf}

Many systems never ask for ratings. They observe instead whether a user bought
an item, liked a post, watched a video past $30$ seconds, or clicked an item when
it was shown. The labels become $1$ (engaged), $0$ (shown, not engaged) and
missing (never shown). Thresholding R54's ratings at $4$ stars gives such a
matrix: Alice and Bob ``like'' Chicken Curry, Carol likes Ginger Cake and Mango
Ice Cream, and so on.

\begin{defbox}{Collaborative filtering with binary labels}
Model the probability of engagement with the sigmoid,
$\Prob(y^{(i,j)} = 1) = \gsig\bigl(\vw^{(j)}\cdot\vx^{(i)} + b^{(j)}\bigr)$, and
replace the squared error by the binary cross-entropy of \cref{ch:ch06}:
\[
  J = \!\!\sum_{(i,j):\,r(i,j)=1}\!\!\Bigl[-y^{(i,j)}\ln f^{(i,j)} - \bigl(1-y^{(i,j)}\bigr)\ln\bigl(1-f^{(i,j)}\bigr)\Bigr]
    + \frac{\lambda}{2}\bigl(\|\mathbf{W}\|^2 + \|\mathbf{X}\|^2\bigr).
\]
\end{defbox}

The step from regression to classification is exactly the step from
\cref{ch:ch05} to \cref{ch:ch06}, and the gradient keeps the same form with
$e^{(i,j)} = f^{(i,j)} - y^{(i,j)}$.

\begin{pitfall}{A zero is not a dislike, and missing is not zero}
``Shown but not clicked'' may mean the user never noticed the item. And items
never shown must be treated as \emph{missing}: recording them as $0$ teaches the
model that whatever it did not recommend is unwanted, and it will go on not
recommending it.
\end{pitfall}

\section{Mean Normalization}
\label{sec:mean-norm}

A new user, Eve, has rated nothing. Her parameters appear only in the penalty, so
gradient descent drives $\vw^{(5)}$ to $\mathbf{0}$, and $b^{(5)}$ --- whose
gradient is an empty sum --- stays at its initial $0$. Every prediction for Eve is
$0$: the system concludes she dislikes everything.

\begin{defbox}{Mean normalization}
Compute each item's mean rating over the users who rated it, $\mu_i$. Subtract it
from that item's ratings before training, and add it back when predicting:
\[
  \hat y^{(i,j)} = \vw^{(j)}\cdot\vx^{(i)} + b^{(j)} + \mu_i .
\]
\end{defbox}

For R54 the dish means are $(2.50,\ 2.50,\ 2.00,\ 2.25,\ 1.67)$. With
normalization Eve's predictions are these means --- the most sensible guess
until her own ratings arrive --- and training also tends to converge faster.
Normalizing by user instead would help a new \emph{item}, which collaborative
filtering cannot otherwise recommend at all.

\section{Finding Related Items}
\label{sec:related}

Collaborative filtering gives every item a feature vector, and items liked by the
same people end up close together. The items most related to item $i$ are
therefore those with the smallest $\|\vx^{(k)} - \vx^{(i)}\|^2$. From the
learned features of \cref{fig:cf-features}, the squared distances from Pepper Soup
are $0.091$ to Chilli Noodles, $0.856$ to Chicken Curry, $7.573$ to Mango Ice
Cream and $7.617$ to Ginger Cake: ``because you ordered Pepper Soup, try the
Chilli Noodles''. No description of any dish was used.

\begin{researchspotlight}{Sarwar et al.\ (2001) --- Item-based collaborative filtering}
\textbf{The problem.} Early recommenders compared each user with every other user
to find similar tastes, which became too slow as user bases grew to millions, and
was unstable because users' tastes change.

\textbf{The idea.} Compare \emph{items} instead: compute similarities between
items from the ratings they received in common, offline, and recommend items
similar to those a user already rated highly.

\textbf{The result.} Item-based methods were faster and, on their benchmarks, more
accurate than user-based ones, and item similarities change slowly enough to be
precomputed. The approach underlies the ``customers who bought this also bought''
features of large online shops.

\textbf{Why it is here.} It is the related-items computation of \cref{sec:related},
with similarities computed directly from co-ratings rather than from learned
feature vectors --- and the idea of precomputing item relationships reappears in
the large-catalogue design of \cref{sec:large-catalogue}.
\parencite{sarwar2001}
\end{researchspotlight}

\section{Content-Based Filtering with Neural Networks}
\label{sec:two-tower}

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{2.6cm}L{4.2cm}L{4.2cm}@{}}
\toprule
 & collaborative filtering & content-based filtering\\
\midrule
uses & ratings of similar users & features of users and items\\
item features & learned from ratings & given: cuisine, price, spice level, photos\\
new item & cannot be recommended until rated & recommended at once\\
new user & needs ratings or normalization & needs profile features\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Collaborative filtering's weakness is the \term{cold-start} problem: it knows
nothing about an item or user until ratings arrive. Content-based filtering uses
side information instead --- a user's age, location and past orders per cuisine;
a dish's cuisine, price, spice level and average rating --- and modern systems do
this with neural networks.

\begin{defbox}{Two-tower model}
A user network maps user features $\vx_u$ to a vector $\mathbf{v}_u$, and an item
network maps item features $\vx_m$ to a vector $\mathbf{v}_m$ of the same length.
The prediction is $\mathbf{v}_u\cdot\mathbf{v}_m$ (or $\gsig(\mathbf{v}_u\cdot
\mathbf{v}_m)$ for binary labels), and both networks are trained together on
\[
  J = \!\!\sum_{(i,j):\,r(i,j)=1}\!\!\bigl(\mathbf{v}_u^{(j)}\cdot\mathbf{v}_m^{(i)} - y^{(i,j)}\bigr)^2
    + \text{penalty} .
\]
\end{defbox}

Related items are again those with nearby vectors, $\|\mathbf{v}_m^{(k)} -
\mathbf{v}_m^{(i)}\|^2$, and because $\mathbf{v}_m$ depends only on the item, every
item's vector can be computed in advance. Normalizing both vectors to unit length,
so that the dot product is a cosine similarity, often makes training more stable.

\begin{researchspotlight}{Covington, Adams \& Sargin (2016) --- Deep neural networks for YouTube recommendations}
\textbf{The problem.} Recommend videos to over a billion users from a corpus of
hundreds of millions, within a strict latency budget, with fresh content
arriving every second.

\textbf{The idea.} Two stages, each a deep network. A \emph{candidate generation}
network embeds users and videos so that a few hundred candidates can be retrieved
by nearest-neighbour search; a \emph{ranking} network scores those candidates with
many more features. The paper also trains on watch time rather than clicks, to
discourage clickbait.

\textbf{The result.} Substantial gains over the previous matrix-factorization
system, and a design that became the template for industrial recommenders.

\textbf{Why it is here.} It is \cref{sec:two-tower} and \cref{sec:large-catalogue}
in production --- and its choice of objective is an instance of
\cref{sec:rec-ethics}: what a system optimises shapes what users see.
\parencite{covington2016}
\end{researchspotlight}

\section{Recommending from a Large Catalogue}
\label{sec:large-catalogue}

Scoring every item for every request does not scale. With $5$ million items and
$32$-dimensional vectors, one request would need $160$ million multiply-adds
before any network is run. Large systems split the work.

\begin{algorithmbox}{Retrieval and ranking}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=pseudocode]
retrieval (cheap, high recall):
    10 most similar items to each of the last 10 orders
    most popular items in the 3 favourite categories
    top items in the user's region
    drop duplicates, items ordered, items unavailable
ranking (accurate, on a few hundred candidates):
    v_u <- user network(x_u)            # once per request
    score each candidate: v_u . v_m     # v_m precomputed
show the top-scoring items
\end{lstlisting}
\end{algorithmbox}

Ranking $500$ candidates costs $16{,}000$ multiply-adds, ten thousand times fewer.
Retrieving more candidates improves recommendations and slows them down; the
number is tuned by offline experiments that measure exactly that trade-off.

\section{Evaluating a Recommender}
\label{sec:rec-eval}

\begin{defbox}{Dataset S80}
Synthetic ratings for $200$ users and $80$ dishes, generated from three latent
tastes plus noise of standard deviation $0.5$. Thirty percent of entries are
observed --- $4796$ ratings --- of which $3837$ are used for training and $959$
held out for testing.
\end{defbox}

set. Root-mean-squared error on the held-out ratings, for mean-normalized

Every idea from \cref{ch:ch07} and \cref{ch:ch10} reappears. One feature
underfits. Three features with a moderate penalty --- the true number of tastes
--- halve the baseline's error. Ten features with no penalty fit the training
ratings almost exactly and are catastrophic on held-out ones: $3.06$, worse than
predicting the mean. A penalty of $1$ rescues them, and a penalty of $10$ is so
strong that the extra features are switched off entirely: with $\lambda = 10$ the
$n = 3$ and $n = 10$ models are identical. $n$ and $\lambda$ are chosen on a
validation set of held-out ratings, exactly as in \cref{ch:ch10}.

\begin{pitfall}{Evaluating by RMSE alone}
Users see a short ranked list, not a matrix of predicted ratings. A model can
improve RMSE by predicting middling ratings for obscure items more accurately
while getting the top of each user's list no better. Evaluate the list itself:
precision and recall among the top $k$ items, and --- since a recommender changes
what users do --- online experiments that measure what users actually choose.
And hold out data by \emph{time}, training on the past and testing on the
future, because a random split lets the model peek at later behaviour.
\end{pitfall}

\section{What Should a Recommender Optimise?}
\label{sec:rec-ethics}

A recommender can be trained to show users the items they are most likely to rate
highly, to buy, to click, or to watch for longest; or the items that earn the most
profit or advertising revenue. These goals often agree, and sometimes do not.

\begin{ethicsnote}{Engagement is a choice, not a neutral target}
Maximising watch time can amplify outrage, conspiracy and extreme content,
because it holds attention. Advertising auctions can reward the businesses that
pay most, not those that serve users best. And recommendations create feedback
loops: what is shown gets clicked, so it is shown more, and the range of what
users ever see narrows. Responsible practice includes being transparent with
users about why an item appears, filtering harmful content and exploitative
sellers, measuring long-term satisfaction rather than only clicks, adding
diversity deliberately, and auditing which items and which creators are never
recommended at all.
\end{ethicsnote}

\section*{Chapter Summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Chapter Summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item A recommendation problem is a ratings matrix with most entries missing; the
        task is to predict them and recommend the highest.
  \item Content-based prediction fits one linear model per user on known item
        features: $\hat y = \vw^{(j)}\cdot\vx^{(i)} + b^{(j)}$.
  \item Collaborative filtering learns item features and user parameters together,
        with the gradient $\mathbf{E}\mathbf{W} + \lambda\mathbf{X}$ for the
        features and $\mathbf{E}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{W}$ for the
        weights. On R54 it recommends Pepper Soup to Alice ($4.62$) and Mango Ice
        Cream to Dave ($3.94$), and separates spicy from sweet dishes unaided.
  \item Binary feedback uses the sigmoid and cross-entropy; unshown items are
        missing, not zero.
  \item Mean normalization gives a new user each item's mean rating instead of
        zero.
  \item Related items have nearby learned vectors: Chilli Noodles is $0.091$ from
        Pepper Soup.
  \item Two-tower networks compute user and item vectors from side information,
        solving cold start; retrieval then ranking makes large catalogues feasible.
  \item Evaluate on held-out ratings: on S80, three features with $\lambda = 1$
        halve the baseline error, while ten unregularized features overfit to
        $3.06$. Evaluate the ranked list too, and split by time.
  \item The objective a recommender optimises is an ethical choice.
\end{itemize}

\furtherreading

\textcite{james2021} §12.3 is the primary reading: it treats a ratings matrix with
missing entries as a matrix-completion problem, solved by iteratively fitting a
low-rank approximation --- the same model as collaborative filtering, derived from
principal components --- and illustrates it on the Netflix data.
\textcite{bishop2006} §12.2 develops probabilistic principal component analysis,
a latent-variable model in which each observation is generated from a small
number of hidden factors; collaborative filtering is a model of this family, and
§12.2 explains in generality why such factors are identifiable only up to
rotation, the point of the pitfall in \cref{sec:collab}.
