Every model so far has been a weighted sum passed through a function: linear
regression, logistic regression, and networks of logistic units. This chapter
introduces a model built on a different principle. A decision tree makes a
prediction by asking a short sequence of yes/no questions about the features,
each question chosen to separate the classes as cleanly as possible.

A single tree is easy to interpret and quick to train, but unstable. Combining
hundreds of trees fixes the instability and produces what remain, in practice,
the strongest general-purpose models for tabular data: random forests and
gradient-boosted trees.

\section{A Tree Is a Sequence of Questions}
\label{sec:tree-idea}

\begin{defbox}{Decision tree}
A \term{decision tree} is a tree of nodes. Each \term{internal node} tests one
feature --- ``is the credit history good?'', ``is the income above $39.5$?'' ---
and sends the example down one of its branches according to the answer. Each
\term{leaf node} holds a prediction: a class for classification, a number for
regression. To classify an example, start at the root and follow the answers to
a leaf.
\end{defbox}

\begin{defbox}{Dataset T11}
Ten past loan applicants, each described by three yes/no features and an income
in thousands of dollars, with the outcome: was the loan repaid?

Learning a tree means deciding which question to ask at each node and when to
stop asking. Both decisions need a measure of how mixed a set of examples is.

\section{Measuring Impurity}
\label{sec:impurity}

\begin{defbox}{Entropy and Gini impurity}
Let $p$ be the fraction of positive examples at a node. Its \term{entropy} is
\[
  H(p) = -p\log_2 p - (1-p)\log_2(1-p), \qquad 0\log_2 0 \equiv 0,
\]
and its \term{Gini impurity} is
\[
  G(p) = 1 - p^2 - (1-p)^2 = 2p(1-p) .
\]
Both are $0$ for a \emph{pure} node ($p = 0$ or $1$) and largest for an even mix
($p = \tfrac12$): $H = 1$, $G = 0.5$.
\end{defbox}

Entropy comes from information theory: it is the average number of bits needed
to transmit the label of an example drawn from the node. A pure node needs none;
a 50/50 node needs one. In practice entropy and Gini almost always choose the
same splits; we use entropy, and \tool{scikit-learn} uses Gini by default.

\begin{derivation}{Information gain}
A split sends a fraction $w_{\text{left}}$ of a node's examples left and
$w_{\text{right}} = 1 - w_{\text{left}}$ right, with positive fractions
$p_{\text{left}}$ and $p_{\text{right}}$. The impurity after the split is the
weighted average of the children's impurities --- weighted because a large child
matters more than a small one. The \term{information gain} is the reduction:
\[
  \text{gain} = H(p_{\text{node}})
    - \bigl(w_{\text{left}}H(p_{\text{left}}) + w_{\text{right}}H(p_{\text{right}})\bigr) .
\]
The weighted average can never exceed $H(p_{\text{node}})$, because $H$ is
concave and $p_{\text{node}} = w_{\text{left}}p_{\text{left}} +
w_{\text{right}}p_{\text{right}}$ (Jensen's inequality), so the gain is never
negative. It is zero exactly when both children have the same mix as the parent
--- a useless question.

At the root of T11, $p = 5/10$ and $H = 1$. Splitting on \emph{good history}
sends five examples left, of which four repaid ($p = 0.8$), and five right, of
which one repaid ($p = 0.2$):
\[
  \text{gain} = 1 - \bigl(\tfrac{5}{10}H(0.8) + \tfrac{5}{10}H(0.2)\bigr)
              = 1 - 0.7219 = 0.2781 .
\]
\end{derivation}

\section{Building a Tree}
\label{sec:build-tree}

The procedure is greedy: at each node, evaluate every possible split, take the
one with the largest information gain, and recurse on the children.

\begin{algorithmbox}{Growing a decision tree}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=pseudocode]
grow(node examples S):
    if S is pure, or a stopping criterion holds:
        return leaf predicting the majority class of S
    for each feature f (each threshold, if numeric):
        compute the information gain of splitting S on f
    choose the split with the largest gain
    split S into children; grow(each child)
\end{lstlisting}
The tree is built top-down, one node at a time, and a split once made is never
revisited. Finding the best tree overall is computationally intractable; greedy
growth is what makes trees fast.
\end{algorithmbox}

Use the three yes/no features (income is treated separately in

\begin{researchspotlight}{Quinlan (1986) --- Induction of decision trees}
\textbf{The problem.} Expert systems of the time were built by interviewing
experts and writing their knowledge down as rules --- slow, expensive, and
inconsistent. Could the rules be induced automatically from examples?

\textbf{The idea.} ID3 grows a tree top-down, choosing at each node the attribute
with the largest information gain, exactly as in \cref{sec:build-tree}. The paper
also discusses noisy data, missing values, and the bias of information gain
towards attributes with many values.

\textbf{The result.} A fast, practical algorithm that produced readable
classifiers from thousands of examples. Its successors, C4.5 and CART (developed
independently by Breiman and colleagues), added continuous features, pruning and
regression trees, and remain the basis of every tree library.

\textbf{Why it is here.} ID3 is the algorithm of this chapter's first half. The
many-values bias it identified is the pitfall of \cref{sec:categorical}.
\parencite{quinlan1986}
\end{researchspotlight}

\section{Continuous Features}
\label{sec:continuous}

A numeric feature such as income is split by a threshold: ``is income above $t$?''

there are nine:

\section{Categorical Features}
\label{sec:categorical}

A feature with $k$ unordered values --- a region, a product category --- can be
split into $k$ branches, or encoded first.

\begin{defbox}{One-hot encoding}
Replace a categorical feature with $k$ values by $k$ binary features, exactly one
of which is $1$. A ``region'' feature with values \emph{north}, \emph{south},
\emph{east} becomes three yes/no features, \emph{is north}, \emph{is south},
\emph{is east}. The same encoding lets categorical features enter linear models
and neural networks.
\end{defbox}

\begin{pitfall}{Information gain loves many-valued features}
A feature with a different value for every example --- an applicant ID, a
timestamp --- splits the training set into pure singletons and has the largest
possible information gain. It is also useless for prediction. Never feed
identifier-like features to a tree; and when categorical features have many
levels, prefer one-hot encoding or a gain measure that penalises many-way splits
(C4.5's \emph{gain ratio}).
\end{pitfall}

\section{When to Stop, and Pruning}
\label{sec:stopping}

The T11 tree split until every leaf was pure, and its two deepest splits each
separated a single pair of applicants. Those splits fit the training data
exactly and almost certainly fit noise: an unemployed applicant with a good
history repaid because they owned a home, according to one example.

Growing to purity is overfitting by the definition of \cref{ch:ch07}. Two
standard controls limit it:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Stopping criteria}: do not split a node if the tree has reached a
        maximum depth, if the node has fewer than a minimum number of examples,
        or if the best gain is below a threshold.
  \item \textbf{Pruning}: grow the full tree, then remove subtrees whose removal
        does not increase validation error (or that a complexity penalty judges
        not worth their size).
\end{itemize}

The maximum depth and minimum leaf size are hyperparameters, chosen on
validation data exactly like $\lambda$ in \cref{ch:ch10}. Pruning tends to work
better than early stopping, because a split that looks weak can enable strong
splits below it.

\section{Regression Trees}
\label{sec:regression-trees}

Trees predict numbers too. A leaf predicts the mean target of its training
examples, and a split is chosen to reduce the \emph{variance} of the targets
instead of the entropy of the labels.

$1.9867$. For each threshold, the reduction is the root variance minus the
weighted variance of the two sides:

\section{One Tree Is Unstable}
\label{sec:instability}

Small changes in the data can change a tree completely, because the choice of
root split depends on small differences in gain, and every split below depends on
it. Resample T11 with replacement --- ten draws from its ten rows, a
\term{bootstrap sample} --- and grow a tree using all four features, a thousand
times. The root feature varies:

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.12}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
\toprule
root split & bootstrap samples (of $1000$)\\
\midrule
income        & $659$\\
good history  & $224$\\
owns home     & $81$\\
employed      & $34$\\
(sample pure) & $2$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A third of the time the tree has a different root, and therefore a completely
different structure. In the language of \cref{ch:ch10}, a fully grown tree is a
low-bias, \emph{high-variance} model. The same holds on larger data: fifty fully
grown trees, each fitted to a bootstrap sample of a $400$-point two-moons
dataset, have test accuracies ranging from $0.8275$ to $0.8705$.

\begin{keyinsight}{Average away the variance}
If $B$ models have independent errors of variance $\sigma^2$, their average has
variance $\sigma^2/B$. Trees grown on different data make partly independent
errors. Averaging many of them keeps the low bias of a deep tree and removes much
of its variance --- the idea behind every method in the rest of the chapter.
\end{keyinsight}

\section{Bagging and Random Forests}
\label{sec:forests}

\begin{algorithmbox}{Bagging (bootstrap aggregating)}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=pseudocode]
for b = 1 to B:
    draw a bootstrap sample: m examples from the
        training set, with replacement
    grow a full decision tree on it
predict: majority vote of the B trees
         (average, for regression)
\end{lstlisting}
\end{algorithmbox}

\begin{defbox}{Random forest}
A \term{random forest} is bagging with one change: at each node, the split is
chosen from a random subset of the features --- typically $\sqrt{n}$ of the $n$
features --- rather than from all of them. This stops every tree from choosing
the same dominant feature at the root, so the trees are more different from one
another, their errors are less correlated, and the average removes more
variance.
\end{defbox}

\begin{workedexample}{Ensembles on two moons}
Two interleaved half-moons with noise: $400$ training and $2000$ test points.

\begin{center}
\small

\midrule

\end{workedexample}

\begin{researchspotlight}{Breiman (2001) --- Random forests}
\textbf{The problem.} Bagging reduced the variance of trees, but trees grown on
bootstrap samples of the same data remain strongly correlated --- they tend to
choose the same strong features --- which limits how much averaging can help.

\textbf{The idea.} Inject a second source of randomness: choose each split from a
random subset of features. Use the examples left out of each bootstrap sample
(about a third) as a built-in validation set --- the \emph{out-of-bag} estimate
--- and measure feature importance by permuting each feature and recording the
loss in accuracy.

\textbf{The result.} An upper bound on the forest's error in terms of the strength
of the individual trees and the correlation between them, and a method that was
accurate across many datasets, robust to noise, and nearly free of tuning.

\textbf{Why it is here.} It is the random forest of \cref{sec:forests}. Its
out-of-bag estimate and permutation importance are still among the most-used
tools for understanding tabular models.
\parencite{breiman2001}
\end{researchspotlight}

\section{Boosting}
\label{sec:boosting}

Bagging grows trees independently and averages them, to reduce variance.
\term{Boosting} grows them \emph{sequentially}, each one fitted to the mistakes
of the ensemble so far, to reduce bias. The trees are usually small --- stumps
(one split) or depth $3$ to $6$.

\begin{algorithmbox}{Gradient boosting for squared error}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=pseudocode]
F_0(x) <- mean of the targets
for t = 1 to T:
    r_i <- y_i - F_{t-1}(x_i)       # residuals
    fit a small regression tree h_t to the r_i
    F_t(x) <- F_{t-1}(x) + eta * h_t(x)
predict F_T(x)
\end{lstlisting}
The learning rate $\eta \in (0,1]$ shrinks each tree's contribution; smaller
$\eta$ needs more trees but generalises better. For squared error the residual
$y - F$ is the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the prediction, so
each round is a gradient-descent step in function space --- hence the name. Other
losses (logistic, absolute) replace the residual by their own negative gradient.
\end{algorithmbox}

\begin{workedexample}{Three rounds of boosting by hand}
Targets $1.0,\ 1.2,\ 2.6,\ 3.0,\ 4.4,\ 4.8$ at $x = 1,\dots,6$; stumps;
$\eta = 0.5$.

$F_0 = 2.8333$, the mean, so the residuals are
$(-1.8333,\ -1.6333,\ -0.2333,\ 0.1667,\ 1.5667,\ 1.9667)$.

\textbf{Round 1.} The best stump on the residuals splits at $x < 4.5$, with leaf
values $-0.8833$ and $1.7667$. Adding half of it:
$F_1 = (2.3917,\ 2.3917,\ 2.3917,\ 2.3917,\ 3.7167,\ 3.7167)$; squared error
$5.4108$.

\textbf{Round 2.} The new residuals are now largest for $x \le 2$. The best stump
splits at $x < 2.5$, with values $-1.2917$ and $0.6458$:
$F_2 = (1.7458,\ 1.7458,\ 2.7146,\ 2.7146,\ 4.0396,\ 4.0396)$; error $1.6569$.

\textbf{Round 3.} A split at $x < 3.5$ separates the middle pair:
$F_3 = (1.5115,\ 1.5115,\ 2.4802,\ 2.9490,\ 4.2740,\ 4.2740)$; error $0.6682$.

Each round attacks the largest remaining error with a different split, building
a staircase up the data.
\end{workedexample}

\begin{pitfall}{Boosting can overfit}
Unlike bagging, adding boosting rounds eventually fits the noise, because each
round fits whatever error remains. On two moons, $200$ boosted stumps reach test
accuracy $0.8835$, while $200$ boosted depth-$3$ trees fit the training set
almost perfectly ($0.9975$) and do worse on test data ($0.8705$). The number of
rounds, the tree depth and the learning rate are hyperparameters, chosen on
validation data --- often with \emph{early stopping} when the validation error
stops falling.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{researchspotlight}{Chen \& Guestrin (2016) --- XGBoost}
\textbf{The problem.} Gradient boosting was accurate but slow on large datasets,
and its behaviour on sparse data and missing values was ad hoc.

\textbf{The idea.} A regularized boosting objective with penalties on the number
of leaves and the size of leaf values; a second-order (Newton) step for each
tree; split finding with sparsity-aware handling of missing values; and systems
engineering --- cache-aware access, out-of-core computation, parallelism --- to
make it fast.

\textbf{The result.} A library that scaled to billions of examples and dominated
machine-learning competitions on structured data for years; LightGBM and
CatBoost followed the same path.

\textbf{Why it is here.} It is the gradient boosting of \cref{sec:boosting} at
industrial strength, and the reason boosted trees are the first model to try on
a table of features.
\parencite{chen2016}
\end{researchspotlight}

\section{Trees or Neural Networks?}
\label{sec:trees-vs-nn}

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{3.2cm}L{3.9cm}L{3.9cm}@{}}
\toprule
 & tree ensembles & neural networks\\
\midrule
data type & tables of features & images, audio, text, and tables\\
training speed & fast & often slow\\
feature scaling & not needed & essential\\
small trees & human-readable & not interpretable\\
transfer learning & not available & central (\cref{ch:ch11})\\
combining with other models & awkward & easy: one differentiable system\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{keyinsight}{The practical default}
For structured, tabular data --- customers, transactions, sensor summaries ---
start with gradient-boosted trees: they are fast, need little preprocessing, and
are very hard to beat. For unstructured data --- pixels, waveforms, words ---
start with a neural network, ideally a pre-trained one. Trees cannot learn the
hierarchical features of \cref{ch:ch08}; networks can.
\end{keyinsight}

\section*{Chapter Summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Chapter Summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item A decision tree classifies by a sequence of feature tests from the root to
        a leaf.
  \item Entropy $H(p)$ and Gini $2p(1-p)$ measure impurity. Information gain is
        the drop in weighted impurity from a split; it is never negative.
  \item Trees are grown greedily: split on the largest gain, recurse. On T11 the
        root is \emph{good history} (gain $0.2781$) among the yes/no features,
        or \emph{income $> 39.5$} (gain $0.6100$) when income is allowed.
  \item Continuous features are split at midpoints between sorted values;
        categorical ones are one-hot encoded. Identifier-like features must be
        excluded.
  \item Growing to purity overfits. Limit depth or leaf size, or prune, with the
        hyperparameters chosen on validation data.
  \item Regression trees split by variance reduction and predict leaf means: a
        step function.
  \item A single tree is high-variance: T11's root split changes in a third of
        bootstrap samples.
  \item Bagging and random forests average many deep trees to cut variance; a
        forest of $200$ trees lifts test accuracy on two moons from $0.8525$ to
        $0.8780$.
  \item Boosting adds small trees sequentially, each fitted to the current
        residuals, to cut bias; it can overfit and needs early stopping.
  \item For tabular data, start with boosted trees; for images, audio and text,
        start with a neural network.
\end{itemize}
