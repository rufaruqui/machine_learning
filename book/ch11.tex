\Cref{ch:ch10} built the measurements: held-out error, bias and variance,

A word on scope. This chapter is about improving a \emph{model}: deciding what to
try next, getting the right data, measuring the right thing. Building and

\label{sec:loop}

Consider building a spam classifier, with $x_j = 1$ if word $j$ of a
$10{,}000$-word vocabulary appears in an email. After a first model, plausible
next steps include: collecting more data, for instance from ``honeypot''
accounts that attract spam; building features from the email's routing headers;
building features from the body, such as punctuation and deliberate misspellings
like \emph{w4tches}; and writing an algorithm to detect those misspellings. Each
could take weeks. The bias/variance diagnosis of \cref{ch:ch10} says whether
more data or more features is the right \emph{kind} of step. It does not say
\emph{which} features. For that, look at the mistakes.

\section{Error Analysis}
\label{sec:error-analysis}

\begin{defbox}{Error analysis}
\term{Error analysis} is the manual inspection of a sample of the examples a
model gets wrong, tagging each with the properties that might explain the error,
and counting the tags. The counts rank the possible improvements by the most
they could achieve.
\end{defbox}

\begin{workedexample}{Error analysis for the spam classifier (illustrative)}
Suppose the classifier misclassifies $500$ of $5000$ validation emails. Sample
$100$ of the $500$ at random, read them, and tag each:

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
\toprule
category & count of $100$\\
\midrule
pharmaceutical spam              & $21$\\
phishing (stealing passwords)    & $18$\\
unusual email routing            & $7$\\
spam inside an embedded image    & $5$\\
deliberate misspellings          & $3$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The categories may overlap; one email can be both pharmaceutical and misspelt.
A perfect misspelling detector would fix at most $3$ of the $100$ --- about $15$
of the $500$ errors. Pharmaceutical spam and phishing together account for $39$.
That is where the next weeks should go: more examples of pharmaceutical spam,
and features for the links that phishing emails contain.
\end{workedexample}

The sample size is a compromise. A hundred is enough to see the large categories
clearly and few enough to read in an hour or two; if the model makes only a few
hundred errors in all, read them all. Error analysis works best on tasks that
people do well --- reading email, recognising objects, transcribing speech ---
because the analyst can see why an error is an error. It is much harder when
people cannot judge the task either, such as predicting which advertisement a
user will click.

\begin{keyinsight}{An error analysis is an upper bound on every idea}
Each category's count is the most that fixing it perfectly could gain. That
turns arguments about which idea is cleverest into arithmetic about which idea
could possibly matter, and it routinely shows that the idea the team was most
excited about addresses three percent of the errors.
\end{keyinsight}

\section{Adding Data}
\label{sec:adding-data}

When the diagnosis is high variance, more data helps. Collecting more of
\emph{everything} is slow and expensive. Three cheaper strategies are usually
available.

\subsection{Targeted collection}

If error analysis says pharmaceutical spam is the problem, collect pharmaceutical
spam: search the unlabelled mail you already have for it, and label only that. A
few hundred targeted examples can do more than tens of thousands of random ones.

\subsection{Data augmentation}

\begin{defbox}{Data augmentation}
\term{Data augmentation} creates new training examples by modifying existing
ones in ways that preserve the label: rotating, scaling, shearing or slightly
distorting an image; adding background noise to a speech recording; replacing
words with synonyms in a sentence.
\end{defbox}

\begin{pitfall}{An augmentation must represent the test distribution}
A distortion helps only if it represents variation the model will meet. Rotated
letters and noisy telephone calls occur in practice; adding independent random
noise to every pixel does not resemble anything a camera produces, and usually
adds little. And an augmentation must not change the label: rotate a $6$ by
$180^\circ$ and it is a $9$.
\end{pitfall}

\subsection{Data synthesis}

\term{Data synthesis} creates examples from scratch. To read text in photographs
--- shop signs, number plates --- render characters in many fonts, sizes, colours
and contrasts on varied backgrounds and crop them: the label is known by
construction. Simulated sensor readings and game-engine images for self-driving
are larger versions of the same idea. The risk is that a model learns the
renderer's quirks instead of the task, so synthetic data should always be
validated on real data, with error analysis checking that it fixes the categories
it was made for.

\subsection{Data-centric development}

There are two ways to spend a week improving a model. \emph{Model-centric}
development holds the data fixed and improves the algorithm. \emph{Data-centric}
development holds a good algorithm fixed and improves the data: fixing
inconsistent labels, collecting targeted examples, augmenting. Now that strong
off-the-shelf architectures exist for most kinds of input, the second is often
the faster route to a better system.

\begin{researchspotlight}{Halevy, Norvig \& Pereira (2009) --- The unreasonable effectiveness of data}
\textbf{The problem.} For tasks such as machine translation and speech
recognition, decades of work on elaborate models had produced modest gains. Was
the bottleneck the model or the data?

\textbf{The idea.} The authors, from a large web company, argued from experience
with web-scale text that simple models with very large amounts of data --- such
as $n$-gram statistics over a trillion words --- outperform sophisticated models
trained on less, and that the right response to the messiness of real data is
to use more of it rather than to model it away.

\textbf{The result.} An influential essay that shifted attention towards data
collection and scale. Its title deliberately echoes Wigner's ``unreasonable
effectiveness of mathematics''.

\textbf{Why it is here.} It is the strongest form of the high-variance remedy of
\cref{ch:ch10}, and the context for \cref{sec:adding-data}. Read it with the
caveat this chapter's learning curves supply: more data helps a high-variance
model and does nothing for a high-bias one.
\parencite{halevy2009}
\end{researchspotlight}

\section{Transfer Learning}
\label{sec:transfer}

Sometimes the data for the task at hand is scarce, but plenty exists for a
related task. \term{Transfer learning} uses the second to help with the first.

\begin{defbox}{Transfer learning: pre-training and fine-tuning}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Supervised pre-training.} Train a network on a large dataset for
        a related task --- for instance, a million photographs labelled with
        $1000$ object classes.
  \item \textbf{Fine-tuning.} Copy all but the output layer to a new network,
        give it a new output layer sized for the task at hand, and continue
        training on the small dataset. Either
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \emph{option 1}: train only the new output layer, keeping the
                copied layers fixed --- best when the new dataset is very small;
                or
          \item \emph{option 2}: train every layer, starting from the copied
                values --- better when there is more data.
        \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}
\end{defbox}

\begin{workedexample}{What option 1 trains}
A network $784 \to 128 \to 64 \to 1000$ is pre-trained on a large image dataset:
$784\cdot128 + 128 = 100{,}480$ parameters in its first layer, $8{,}256$ in its
second, and $65{,}000$ in its $1000$-class output layer. To recognise ten digits,
replace the output layer with a $64\to10$ layer of $650$ parameters. Option 1
trains those $650$ of the network's $109{,}386$ --- $0.59\%$ --- and the
$108{,}736$ copied parameters supply everything else.
\end{workedexample}

It works because the early layers of a network trained on images learn generic
features --- edges, corners, simple shapes (\cref{sec:learned-features}) --- that
are useful for almost any image task. The pre-training input must therefore be
of the same \emph{type} as the new task's: images for an image task, audio for a
speech task, text for a language task. A network pre-trained on photographs has
nothing to offer a table of loan applications. In practice the pre-trained
network is usually downloaded, not trained: researchers and companies publish
networks trained on very large datasets. The large language models of
\cref{ch:ch19} are the same idea at the largest scale yet.

\section{Skewed Classes}
\label{sec:skew}

Many important classification problems are badly imbalanced: a rare disease, a
fraudulent transaction, a failing machine. On such data, accuracy is not merely
uninformative but actively misleading.

\begin{defbox}{Confusion matrix}
For a binary classifier, the \term{confusion matrix} counts the four outcomes:

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l|cc@{}}
 & actual $1$ & actual $0$\\
\hline
predicted $1$ & true positives (TP) & false positives (FP)\\
predicted $0$ & false negatives (FN) & true negatives (TN)\\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
By convention the rare class is labelled $y = 1$.
\end{defbox}

\begin{workedexample}{The accuracy trap at $0.5\%$ prevalence}
A screening programme tests $10{,}000$ people, of whom $50$ have the disease. A
classifier produces TP $= 35$, FN $= 15$, FP $= 90$, TN $= 9{,}860$. Its accuracy
is $(35 + 9{,}860)/10{,}000 = 0.9895$.

The program that prints ``healthy'' for everyone is right about the $9{,}950$
healthy people and wrong about the $50$ sick ones: accuracy $0.9950$. It is more
accurate than the classifier, and it never finds a single case.
\end{workedexample}

\section{Precision, Recall and \texorpdfstring{$F_1$}{F1}}
\label{sec:precision-recall}

\begin{defbox}{Precision and recall}
\[
  \text{precision} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FP}}, \qquad
  \text{recall} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FN}} .
\]
\term{Precision} is the fraction of the examples flagged positive that really
are positive. \term{Recall} is the fraction of the positives that were flagged.
\end{defbox}

For the screening classifier above, precision is $35/125 = 0.28$ and recall is
$35/50 = 0.70$: it finds seven in ten cases, and seven in ten of the people it
flags are healthy. The ``always healthy'' program has recall $0$ and an undefined
precision ($0/0$). The two numbers expose it at once, which is the point of them.

\subsection{The trade-off}

A logistic model produces a probability, and the threshold turns it into a
decision (\cref{sec:threshold}). Raising the threshold flags fewer cases, more
confidently: precision rises and recall falls. Lowering it does the reverse.

\begin{defbox}{Screening model S}
To see the trade-off in full, consider a screening score with
$\mathcal{N}(3,1)$ distribution among the sick and $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ among the
healthy, prevalence $0.5\%$, and a classifier that reports the exact
probability $\Prob(y = 1 \mid \text{score})$. Everything below is computed
exactly from the normal distribution, per $10{,}000$ people screened.
\end{defbox}

\subsection{Combining the two}

Two numbers are awkward for choosing among models. The obvious single number,
their average, is a trap.

\begin{defbox}{$F_1$ score}
\[
  F_1 = \frac{1}{\tfrac12\bigl(\tfrac1P + \tfrac1R\bigr)} = \frac{2PR}{P + R},
\]
the \term{harmonic mean} of precision $P$ and recall $R$. It is dominated by the
smaller of the two, so a model must do reasonably well on both to score well.
\end{defbox}

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
 & $P$ & $R$ & mean & $F_1$\\
\midrule
algorithm 1 & $0.5$  & $0.4$ & $0.450$ & $\mathbf{0.4444}$\\
algorithm 2 & $0.7$  & $0.1$ & $0.400$ & $0.1750$\\
algorithm 3 & $0.02$ & $1.0$ & $\mathbf{0.510}$ & $0.0392$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Algorithm 3 flags almost everyone: perfect recall, useless precision. The mean
ranks it first; $F_1$ ranks it last. The screening classifier of
\cref{sec:skew} has $F_1 = 2(0.28)(0.70)/0.98 = 0.40$, and model S reaches its
best $F_1$, $0.566$, at a threshold of about $0.28$ --- not at the default $0.5$.

\section{Choosing a Threshold from Costs}
\label{sec:threshold-costs}

$F_1$ weights the two kinds of error as if they cost the same. In screening they
emphatically do not, and when the costs are known the threshold can be derived
rather than tuned.

\begin{derivation}{The cost-minimising threshold}
Let a false negative cost $c_{\text{FN}}$ and a false positive $c_{\text{FP}}$.
For an example with predicted probability $p = \Prob(y = 1\mid\vx)$, assumed
calibrated (\cref{sec:calibration}), the expected cost of each decision is
\[
  \text{predict } 0:\ p\,c_{\text{FN}}, \qquad
  \text{predict } 1:\ (1-p)\,c_{\text{FP}} .
\]
Predict $1$ when the second is smaller:
\[
  (1-p)\,c_{\text{FP}} < p\,c_{\text{FN}}
  \iff
  p > \boxed{\;t^\star = \frac{c_{\text{FP}}}{c_{\text{FP}} + c_{\text{FN}}}\;}
\]
Equal costs give $t^\star = \tfrac12$, the default. A missed case that costs as
much as fifty false alarms gives $t^\star = 1/51 = 0.0196$. The threshold
depends only on the cost ratio, and the derivation needs the probabilities to be
calibrated: an overconfident model applied with this threshold makes the wrong
trade.
\end{derivation}

\begin{workedexample}{Pricing the errors for model S}
With $c_{\text{FN}} = 50$ and $c_{\text{FP}} = 1$, the expected cost per
$10{,}000$ people screened is $50\,\text{FN} + \text{FP}$:

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrr@{}}
\toprule
threshold & FN & FP & cost\\
\midrule
$0.0050$ & $3.34$  & $664.7$ & $831.7$\\
$0.0196$ & $7.46$  & $248.6$ & $\mathbf{621.7}$\\
$0.0500$ & $11.83$ & $111.6$ & $703.3$\\
$0.1000$ & $16.00$ & $56.4$  & $856.2$\\
$0.2832$ (best $F_1$) & $24.10$ & $15.6$ & $1220.6$\\
$0.5000$ & $30.21$ & $5.5$   & $1516.2$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A search over every threshold confirms the minimum at $t^\star = 0.0196$. The
threshold that maximises $F_1$ costs almost exactly twice as much.
\end{workedexample}

\begin{pitfall}{Optimising $F_1$ when the two errors cost differently}
$F_1$ is a convenient single number for comparing models when nobody has priced
the errors. Once someone has, use the cost. Here the best-$F_1$ threshold misses
$24$ cases per $10{,}000$ where the cost-optimal one misses $7.5$, because $F_1$
cannot know that a missed case is worth fifty false alarms. The weighted
$F_\beta = (1+\beta^2)PR/(\beta^2P + R)$ puts more weight on recall for
$\beta > 1$, but an explicit cost is always clearer.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{ethicsnote}{Who sets the cost ratio?}
The ratio $c_{\text{FN}}/c_{\text{FP}}$ decides how many healthy people are
recalled to find one more case. It is a value judgement about competing harms
--- anxiety and unnecessary procedures on one side, missed disease on the other
--- and it belongs to the people who bear those harms and to those accountable
for them, not to whoever happens to write the code. Record the ratio, who chose
it and why, next to the model.
\end{ethicsnote}

\section{Fairness: Evaluating for Everyone}
\label{sec:fairness}

A model's error rates are averages, and an average can hide a group the model
serves badly. That matters most when the model makes consequential decisions
about people.

\subsection{Sources of harm}

Harm usually enters through the data. A group under-represented in the training
set gets a model that has seen too few of its examples. Labels that record past
human decisions --- who was hired, who was granted a loan, who was arrested ---
encode whatever bias those decisions contained, and a model trained on them
learns to reproduce it at scale. Features that look neutral can stand in for
protected attributes: a postal code can carry much of the information in race or
income. And a model can be used for purposes its builders never evaluated it for.

\subsection{Subgroup evaluation}

The first defence is simply to measure. Report every metric separately for each
group the model will be applied to.

\begin{workedexample}{One threshold, two groups}
A screening model is applied to two groups with the same prevalence, $5\%$. For
group A the score separates the classes as before (positives $\mathcal{N}(3,1)$);
for group B, under-represented in the training data, it separates them less well
(positives $\mathcal{N}(2.2,1)$). A single threshold, set at the score where
group A's probability is $0.5$, is applied to both:

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrrr@{}}
\toprule
group & recall & miss rate & false-positive rate & precision & accuracy\\
\midrule
A & $0.698$ & $0.302$ & $0.0065$ & $0.849$ & $0.9787$\\
B & $0.389$ & $0.611$ & $0.0065$ & $0.758$ & $0.9632$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Group B's cases are missed twice as often as group A's, $61\%$ against $30\%$.
If B is $10\%$ of the population, the pooled accuracy is $0.9771$, which looks
excellent and reveals nothing.

Lowering B's threshold until its recall matches A's, $0.698$, raises B's
false-positive rate from $0.0065$ to $0.0463$ --- seven times as many healthy
members of B recalled. Equalising one error rate unbalanced the other. The real
remedy is upstream: better data and features for group B, so that its score
separates the classes as well as A's does.
\end{workedexample}

\begin{keyinsight}{Fairness criteria compete}
``Equal recall for every group'', ``equal false-positive rate'' and ``equally
calibrated probabilities'' are each a reasonable definition of a fair
classifier, and when groups differ in base rates or in how well the model
separates them, no threshold satisfies all of them at once. Choosing among them
is a decision about values, not a technical detail; the technical work is to
measure all of them, report them, and reduce the underlying disparity in model
quality.
\end{keyinsight}

\begin{researchspotlight}{Buolamwini \& Gebru (2018) --- Gender Shades}
\textbf{The problem.} Commercial face-analysis systems were reported to be highly
accurate. Were they equally accurate for everyone?

\textbf{The idea.} Build a benchmark balanced by gender and by skin type, and
evaluate three commercial gender-classification systems on each intersectional
subgroup separately, rather than on the population as a whole.

\textbf{The result.} All three systems performed worst on darker-skinned women,
with error rates up to $34.7\%$, against at most $0.8\%$ for lighter-skinned
men. The headline accuracy figures had concealed a disparity of more than forty
to one. Several of the companies improved their systems after publication.

\textbf{Why it is here.} It is subgroup evaluation, \cref{sec:fairness}, done on
production systems, and it showed that the disparity was invisible precisely
because nobody had measured by subgroup. The benchmark's design --- balanced by
construction, so that small groups are measured precisely --- is a lesson in
itself.
\parencite{buolamwini2018}
\end{researchspotlight}

\subsection{Model cards}

\begin{defbox}{Model card}
A \term{model card} is a short document that travels with a trained model and
records: what the model is for and what it must not be used for; the data it was
trained and evaluated on; its performance, overall and \emph{for each relevant
group}, with the threshold used; the cost ratio behind that threshold and who
chose it; known limitations and failure modes; and the people responsible for
it.
\end{defbox}

\begin{researchspotlight}{Mitchell et al.\ (2019) --- Model cards for model reporting}
\textbf{The problem.} Trained models were being released and reused with little
information about how they had been evaluated, for whom they worked, or where
they should not be used.

\textbf{The idea.} A standard, short report to accompany every model: intended
use, factors such as demographic groups, metrics, evaluation and training data,
quantitative analyses disaggregated by group, ethical considerations, and
caveats --- analogous to a datasheet for an electronic component.

\textbf{The result.} A widely adopted documentation practice; many published
models now ship with a model card, and the format has been extended to datasets
and to whole systems.

\textbf{Why it is here.} It turns the measurements of this chapter --- subgroup
metrics, the threshold, the cost ratio --- into a record someone else can audit.
\Cref{ch:ch22} returns to it as part of operating a deployed model.
\parencite{mitchellm2019}
\end{researchspotlight}

\begin{ethicsnote}{Before you build}
Some harms are best prevented before any data is collected. Assemble a team
with varied backgrounds to brainstorm how the system could fail or be misused,
especially for vulnerable groups. Look for established standards in the
application area --- medicine, lending and hiring all have them. Audit for harm
before deployment, with the subgroup measurements above. Prepare a plan for
rolling back if harm appears after release, and keep monitoring after
deployment, because the population a model serves changes. And be willing to
conclude that a system should not be built.
\end{ethicsnote}

\section*{Chapter Summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Chapter Summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Model development is a loop of choosing, training and diagnosing. The
        diagnosis decides how many turns a project needs.
  \item Error analysis tags a sample of the errors by hand. Each category's count
        is an upper bound on what fixing it can gain: a perfect misspelling
        detector could fix $3\%$ of the spam errors.
  \item More data helps high variance. Collect it where error analysis points;
        augment with distortions that represent the test distribution and keep
        the label; synthesise when the input can be rendered.
  \item Transfer learning pre-trains on a large related task and fine-tunes;
        option 1 trains only the new output layer ($650$ of $109{,}386$
        parameters in the example).
  \item At $0.5\%$ prevalence the ``always negative'' rule has accuracy $0.9950$,
        beating a classifier that finds $70\%$ of cases. Use precision, recall
        and $F_1 = 2PR/(P+R)$.
  \item The threshold trades precision for recall. Model S's best $F_1$ is
        $0.566$ at a threshold of $0.28$.
  \item When the errors have costs, the cost-minimising threshold is
        $c_{\text{FP}}/(c_{\text{FP}} + c_{\text{FN}})$ for calibrated
        probabilities --- $0.0196$ when a miss costs fifty false alarms, half
        the cost of the best-$F_1$ threshold.
  \item Evaluate every metric per group. One threshold missed $61\%$ of one
        group's cases and $30\%$ of another's; equalising recall raised the
        first group's false-positive rate sevenfold. Fairness criteria compete.
  \item A model card records intended use, data, per-group performance, the
        threshold and its cost ratio, limitations, and who is responsible.
\end{itemize}

\textcite{james2021} §4.4.2 is the primary reading: working through a
credit-default example with heavily imbalanced classes, it builds the confusion
matrix, shows how lowering the threshold trades one error for the other, defines
sensitivity and specificity, and introduces the ROC curve --- the companion of
\cref{fig:pr-curve}, which plots recall against the false-positive rate instead
of precision. \textcite{bishop2006} §1.5 is the supplementary reading for
\cref{sec:threshold-costs}: it sets classification as decision theory, with a
loss matrix for unequal error costs, derives the decision rule that minimises
expected loss, and discusses the \emph{reject option} of deferring the least
certain cases to a human --- a practical response to the trade-offs of this
chapter.
